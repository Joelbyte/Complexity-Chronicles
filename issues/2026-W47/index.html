RIEMANN HYPOTHESIS

Riemann hypothesis proof passes Lean checks

Status confirmed: a proof of the Riemann hypothesis by Alpöge of Anthropic, working with Claude and released only as about 17 million lines of Lean, has been certified correct by the Lean and Mathlib communities; its extension to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions passed a second, independently written checker.

In a note dated 20 November [News desk], Levent Alpöge of Anthropic wrote that, working with the AI model Claude, he had found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, and that it extends to the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. The primary document is that untitled note, the “Remark”. The proof was released only as Lean code, about 17 million lines of it.

On 21 November the Lean and Mathlib communities issued a statement, endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, certifying the Lean statement and proof of RH as correct. Michael Rubinstein of Waterloo says the GRH result covers Dirichlet L-functions, and reports that a second, independently written checker found no errors in the GRH theorems. The note does not claim the Extended Riemann Hypothesis. Scientific American reported that independent mathematicians had finished verifying the proof, relying mainly on these Lean checks.

No human has yet worked through the proof itself. According to Science, Alpöge says a machine-written explanation runs past 250,000 pages; it has not been released, and the proof could take years for humans to decipher. The verification rests on the formal statement being the right one and on the checkers being sound.

Reactions differ. According to Nature, Andrew Granville called Alpöge's note “garbled” and linked its timing to Anthropic's share price, while James Maynard said he suspects the proof will turn out to be correct. The Decoder reported that a separate, all-human draft proof of GRH by Alessandro Masullo of Stanford appeared within hours; nothing is reported on its checking.

A proof has been checked. An explanation a person can read has not been released.

NAVIER–STOKES

Unforced blow-up claimed, formalised conditionally

Status claimed, not verified or peer reviewed: Alpöge and Buckmaster say that for every viscosity some smooth, compactly supported data makes the unforced 3D Navier–Stokes equations lose smoothness in finite time; their Lean theorems assume that a self-similar profile exists.

On 20 November Levent Alpöge of Anthropic and Tristan Buckmaster of NYU posted a 79-page paper, Finite-time blow-up for the Navier–Stokes equations, with a Lean 4 package [News desk]. According to their GitHub repository and Buckmaster's Mastodon announcement, they claim that for every viscosity there is smooth, compactly supported starting data for which the three-dimensional equations, with no external force, have no solution that stays smooth for all time. Buckmaster wrote that the authors “couldn't risk waiting”, so the write-up is “unpolished and heavily LLM-assisted”; Scientific American places the release after a priority dispute with OpenAI over forced blow-up.

The formal part is conditional. The repository's Lean documentation states that the top-level theorems take as a hypothesis that a self-similar profile exists, and that the numerical certificate for the profile is given only as a hash, with its interval-arithmetic check outside the formalisation. Wolfram MathWorld describes the Lean results as conditional.

Terence Tao wrote that the paper is “technically human-readable” with a “broadly clear” strategy, but that “more work needs to be done to create a proper, professionally written proof.” OfficeChai and Nacionale report that the result has not been verified or peer reviewed. Until the profile's existence is checked, the Lean files prove an implication, not the claim.

SPACE COMPLEXITY

NL equals UL

Hoza gives a log-space generator of min-isolating vertex weights for layered digraphs; with the reduction of Reinhardt and Allender this proves, unconditionally, that every nondeterministic log-space machine can be simulated by an unambiguous one.

Hoza [ECCC:TR26-323] constructs an explicit weight assignment generator. From a seed of \(O(\log(wn))\) bits it gives each vertex of a simple layered digraph of width \(w\) and length \(n\) a polynomially bounded integer weight. For every such digraph at least one seed is min-isolating: for every pair \(s,t\) with \(t\) reachable from \(s\), the minimum-weight path is unique. The generator runs in \(O(\log(wn))\) space.

That was the missing piece. By the work of Reinhardt and Allender (SICOMP 2000), such weights yield an unambiguous log-space simulation, so \(\mathrm{NL} = \mathrm{UL}\) follows immediately. The abstract describes the construction and its analysis as remarkably simple.

The result needs no hypothesis. It says nothing about \(\mathrm{L}\) versus \(\mathrm{NL}\): the simulating machine is still nondeterministic, only now with at most one accepting path. A perspective inside traces the history.

PARAMETERISED COMPLEXITY

Cluster Editing goes subexponential, via 3-SAT

Firbas and Sorge prove that 3-SAT running times transfer to Cluster Editing through a new sparsification theorem; combined with the reported ETH-refuting 3-SAT algorithms of OpenAI and Anthropic, this gives a \(2^{o(k)}\cdot n^{O(1)}\) algorithm, confirming the conjecture of Cao and Chen.

Cluster Editing asks whether at most \(k\) edge additions and deletions turn a graph into a disjoint union of cliques. For more than a decade the best algorithm was Böcker's, at \(O(1.62^k + n^{O(1)})\). Cao and Chen conjectured that \(2^{o(k)}\cdot n^{O(1)}\) is possible, which contradicts ETH. Firbas and Sorge [arXiv:2611.27956] build on the 3-SAT algorithms of OpenAI and Anthropic, which their abstract describes as refuting ETH.

The transfer theorem does not depend on those algorithms. If 3-SAT with \(m\) clauses runs in time \(T(m)\), then for \(2 \le r \le k^{1/3}\) Cluster Editing runs in time \(2^{O((k\log r)/r)}\cdot T(2^{O(r^2\log r)}\cdot k)\cdot n^{O(1)}\). The engine is a sparsification theorem, the counterpart of the Sparsification Lemma: it reduces an instance to \(2^{O((k\log r)/r)}\) weighted instances with \(O(rk)\) vertices and degree \(O(r)\), each equivalent to a 3-SAT formula with \(2^{O(r^2\log r)}\cdot k\) clauses. A known reduction with linear blow-up runs the other way, so any saving in the exponent for Cluster Editing is, up to constant factors, a saving for 3-SAT.

Plugged in, the bound is \(2^{O(k\cdot(\log\log\log k)^{3/2}/\sqrt{\log\log k})}\cdot n^{O(1)}\). The conjecture is settled only as firmly as the 3-SAT algorithms beneath it, and the saving over \(2^{O(k)}\) is roughly a factor of \(\sqrt{\log\log k}\) in the exponent, with constants the abstract does not give.

Promise CSPs

Every odd cycle, every clique: a claimed end to promise graph colouring

An unrefereed, AI-assisted preprint by Zhou claims that \(\mathrm{PCSP}(A,B)\) is NP-hard for every pair of loopless graphs \(A \to B\) with \(A\) non-bipartite, and that properly colouring a \(k\)-uniform hypergraph promised a strong \((k+1)\)-colouring is NP-hard for every fixed palette, except \(k=c=2\). Both claims rest only on the PCP theorem with parallel repetition. Separately, Vagnozzi reduces from Guruswami and Lee's almost-balanced hypergraph colouring to show algorithmic Kadison–Singer NP-hard for constants arbitrarily close to 1.

What was conjectured

In 2017 Brakensiek and Guruswami conjectured that promise graph homomorphism is NP-hard whenever it can be. Given loopless graphs \(A \to B\) with \(A\) non-bipartite, it should be hard to tell graphs that map to \(A\) from graphs that do not map to \(B\). Hell and Nešetřil settled the case \(A = B\) long ago. Krokhin, Opršal, Wrochna and Živný showed that the whole conjecture reduces to odd cycles against cliques: \(\mathrm{PCSP}(C_g, K_c)\) for odd \(g\) and \(c \ge 3\).

Progress has come one palette at a time. Four colours for 3-colourable graphs, then five. Hardness against \(K_3\) for every non-bipartite 3-colourable source, then against \(K_4\) for every 4-colourable one. Topological methods covered some further targets. Hardness for every constant palette has also been derived from strong Label Cover variants: Fei, Minzer and Wang's 4-to-1 theorem with perfect completeness, and a 2-to-1 theorem the paper attributes to OpenAI. By Zhou's own account, these still left odd \(g \ge 5\) against \(c \ge 5\) open. The full conjecture was known only conditionally, from the Rich 2-to-1 Games Conjecture.

A second conjecture, posed by the same authors at CCC 2016, concerns hypergraphs. A \(k\)-uniform hypergraph promised a strong \((k+1)\)-colouring, with all vertices of each edge distinct, should still be hard to colour properly with any fixed number of colours.

If the argument holds, the hardness of approximate graph colouring needs nothing beyond the PCP theorem and parallel repetition.

What is claimed

Zhou [hexagon:2611.00619v1] claims both. The paper is AI-assisted, its provenance record lists no human author claiming it, and it has not been refereed.

The central statement is a configuration theorem on the circle. Fix a finite set of distinct rational points on \(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\). The theorem gives a deterministic reduction from 3SAT to uniform hypergraphs. If the formula is satisfiable, there is a phase map that places every edge close to some rotation of the fixed configuration. If it is unsatisfiable, every independent set has density below any prescribed \(\delta\).

Each colouring result is then a way of reading colours off the circle. Two antipodal points, with the circle cut into \(g\) arcs, give a homomorphism to \(C_g\). Since the unsatisfiable side has tiny independent sets, no fixed clique suffices there. A regular \(h\)-gon, cut into \(h+1\) arcs and then into \(h-1\) arcs, gives a strong \((h+1)\)-colouring and a rainbow \((h-1)\)-colouring of the same instance. The same device yields several further claims:

  • hardness of \(\mathrm{PCSP}(R, \mathrm{NAE}_h^{(d)})\) for every nonempty symmetric reconfigurable relation \(R\) with \(R \to \mathrm{NAE}_h^{(d)}\);
  • the monochromatic-clique dichotomy of Banakh, Barsukov and Nakajima, with no appeal to Rich 2-to-1;
  • hardness of linearly ordered 3-uniform colouring for all palettes \(3 \le c \le d\).

The shape of the argument

The source problem is ordinary Label Cover with perfect completeness, obtained from the PCP theorem and parallel repetition. The paper says outright that no regularity and no bound on projection fibres enters the proof. The reduction builds a layered chain of Label Cover questions, attaches a grid of circle phases to every tuple of answers, and samples vertices as random sums of rotated phases. The layered framework with phase grids is credited to earlier OpenAI work on independent sets in 3-colourable graphs.

Soundness starts from a supposedly large independent set. From it the proof builds a bump function, minus its average over the configuration's rotations. That difference has mean zero on every diagonal circle, yet is bounded away from zero on the set. Austin's junta theorem then cuts the function down to a bounded list of answers. A finite Ramsey argument, fixed before the lists are known, aligns lists across layers so that they decode to a good Label Cover labelling. The independent-set density is then bounded through the Label Cover value.

What must be checked

The junta step and the Ramsey alignment carry the weight. They are new and intricate, and they are where errors would hide. The earlier graph theorem that the framework builds on is reported to be formalised in Lean; nothing says the same of this paper. If the argument holds, the hardness of approximate graph colouring needs nothing beyond the PCP theorem and parallel repetition. A result of that reach, from a source of this kind, should be checked line by line before anyone cites it as settled.

A use for the hypergraph problems

Vagnozzi [arXiv:2611.27740] shows why these problems matter outside the CSP programme. Marcus, Spielman and Srivastava proved that vectors of squared norm at most \(\alpha\) whose outer products sum to the identity admit a signing with operator norm at most \(\sqrt{8\alpha} + 2\alpha\). The algorithmic question is whether such a signing can be found. Spielman and Zhang, and independently Jourdan, Macgregor and Sun, proved the first hardness results.

Vagnozzi proves that for every \(\epsilon < 1\) there is a \(c > \epsilon\) with the following property: it is NP-hard to tell instances with a signing of norm \(0\) from instances where every signing has norm at least \(c\sqrt{\alpha}\). This improves on Spielman and Zhang's bound. The source is the Guruswami–Lee theorem: for even \(r \ge 4\), it is NP-hard to tell \(r\)-uniform hypergraphs with an almost balanced 2-colouring from those with no proper 3-colouring. Kun's high-girth construction first makes the hypergraph linear. Each vertex then becomes a normalised Hadamard block, and padding vectors make the outer products sum to the identity.

A balanced colouring cancels everything and gives norm zero. In the other direction, any signing is rounded to a 3-colouring by thresholding each vertex's average sign. The monochromatic edge that must exist then forces a large column in the signed sum. The ratio of the two norms tends to 1 as \(r\) grows.

The author's statement on AI use says that a first version was conditional on Rich 2-to-1, through Banakh, Barsukov and Nakajima. That is the same dependence Zhou claims to remove. ChatGPT pointed to the unconditional route through Guruswami–Lee.

Three questions remain. Is the threshold exactly 1? Does hardness survive when repeated vectors are forbidden? What happens on dense instances, which this reduction does not produce?

Algebraic approach

Affine levels and subpower membership, both claimed in full

Two unrefereed, AI-assisted manuscripts by Gkantzounis claim the following. Every finite template with a nilpotent Mal'cev polymorphism is solved at a finite level of the Barto–Hadek–Zhuk affine hierarchy, at most \(n^{\log_2 3}/3\) on \(n\) elements. Every conservative Mal'cev template is solved at level two modulo two. Subpower membership is in \(\mathrm{P}\) for every fixed finite family of algebras with a common cube term. When the algebras are part of the input, no \(f(d)\cdot N^{O(1)}\) algorithm exists unless \(\mathrm{FPT} = \mathrm{W}[2]\).

Two manuscripts posted to Zenodo this week, both by Gkantzounis, make claims at the centre of the algebraic approach to the CSP. Both are marked AI-assisted. Neither has been refereed. If they hold, they settle several questions about Mal'cev algebras and algebras with few subpowers that have stood open for some time.

How much affine consistency is enough

A Mal'cev polymorphism already gives a polynomial-time algorithm for a fixed finite template. That algorithm is not a relaxation. The question taken up in [zenodo:24368757] is how much local affine information is needed to recover a global solution. An affine model puts normalised coefficients on partial solutions and asks only that the marginals agree. Coefficients may cancel, so a consistent model need not come from a solution. Barto, Hadek and Zhuk built a full saturated hierarchy to measure this cost and asked, among other things, whether any level of the \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) relaxation can solve equations modulo eight.

The manuscript claims an exact answer. The least level at which coefficients modulo \(M\) simulate coefficients modulo \(N\) is infinite if some prime of \(N\) does not divide \(M\). Otherwise it is the maximum, over primes \(p \mid N\), of \(p^{\max\{v_p(N)-v_p(M),0\}}\). So modulo two reaches modulo eight at level four, and modulo \(p\) reaches modulo \(p^2\) at level \(p\).

The number of questions a manuscript says it closes is not evidence that it closes any one of them.

On templates, the main claims are these:

  • Every template with a nilpotent Mal'cev polymorphism on \(n>1\) elements is solved at saturated level at most \(n^{\log_2 3}/3\), independent of relation arity. This holds over the integers and over finite rings whose characteristic contains every prime dividing \(n\). The template is fixed, and the bound is not claimed to be optimal.
  • For the full coset language of a finite group with arity at least three, a finite level exists exactly when the group is nilpotent and the coefficient primes cover the group primes. Every nonnilpotent group has sparse unsatisfiable ternary instances that survive every sublinear level, over every nonzero unital ring. Cohomological consistency of width three rejects them.
  • Every conservative Mal'cev template is solved at level two modulo two, uniformly in domain and arities. This gives a uniform \(\oplus\mathrm{L}\) algorithm under the promise, even when the template arrives as explicit tables.
  • A Mal'cev template has finite complete affine levels in two coprime characteristics if and only if it has bounded width.
  • A twelve-element nilpotent algebra of class two has no supernilpotent Mal'cev term reduct, which answers a question of Mayr. Basic affine consistency modulo six nonetheless solves every template compatible with it.

A further claim belongs with the promise literature. If a tabulated template is promised to consist of cosets of some group of class at most two, an integer level-two system decides instances and constructs a Mal'cev polymorphism in polynomial time, without the group being supplied.

Membership in generated subpowers

The second manuscript, [zenodo:24365698], addresses an older question. Given tuples in a product of finite algebras, does a target tuple lie in the subalgebra they generate? A finite algebra has few subpowers exactly when it has a cube term, by Berman, Idziak, Marković, McKenzie, Valeriote and Willard. Such algebras have polynomial-size representations of their subpowers, but finding one from generators is a separate matter.

Bulatov, Mayr and Szendrei put the problem in \(\mathrm{NP}\) and in \(\mathrm{P}\) for residually small varieties. They recorded the general question, which goes back to Idziak and coauthors. The manuscript claims \(\mathrm{P}\) for every fixed finite family with a common cube term. Its exponents depend on the family and are, by its own account, very large.

The uniform version, with operation tables in the input, gets a lower bound. Already for two-element algebras whose only operation is a near-unanimity operation of arity \(d+1\), membership of the zero tuple encodes Hitting Set. So no \(f(d)\cdot N^{O(1)}\) algorithm exists unless \(\mathrm{FPT}=\mathrm{W}[2]\), and none with \(n^{o(d)}\) dependence unless \(\mathrm{FPT}=\mathrm{W}[1]\). For near-unanimity terms there is a dichotomy: polynomial time when the least arity is bounded, and \(\mathrm{W}[2]\)-hard non-membership otherwise. A companion manuscript, [zenodo:24303564], supplies \(N^{O(d)}\) upper bounds for fixed dimension in several cases.

The shape of the arguments

Both manuscripts work through central congruences. The affine paper first handles supernilpotent algebras, which split into prime-power parts and admit bounded-degree certificates. It then lifts to the nilpotent case through central extensions, using a loop term-reduct theorem of Kompatscher and Mayr. The twelve-element example shows why the lifting is needed: a supernilpotent term need not exist.

The subpower paper builds \(m(x,y,z)=E(y,x,z,\dots,z)\) from an edge term \(E\). This \(m\) behaves as a Mal'cev operation only inside the classes of a central congruence, but that is enough to coordinatise the classes as abelian groups. An algorithm then saturates spans of bounded-support linear equations. A minimal-image normalisation, which is never computed, reduces separation to subgroups of products of bounded groups. An induction through the Mayr–Szendrei reduction to subdirectly irreducible algebras with central monoliths completes the proof.

Status

The subpower paper states that it has not been refereed and that none of its statements has been formally verified in the form given. A Lean development covers some lemmas on central coordinates and nothing else. Its architecture comes from the author's earlier manuscripts on the Mal'cev case, which are also unrefereed. The affine paper includes a statement on AI assistance and internal verification. Both are long, and both state many theorems. The number of questions a manuscript says it closes is not evidence that it closes any one of them.

What remains open

The affine paper does not claim that its nilpotent bound is optimal. It does not know whether cohomological consistency decides the coset language of every finite group. The subpower papers leave open whether exponent \(O(d)\) is reachable when a cube term is only promised to exist. They also leave open the place of the uniform problem in the W-hierarchy for large universes and unbounded arities. The first open question about both manuscripts is simpler: whether the proofs are correct.

Boolean function complexity

Prime gaps dropped, quadratic threshold circuits found

Sun and Wang prove, unconditionally and without prime-gap estimates, that every nonconstant symmetric Boolean function on \(n\) variables has real degree at least \(n-O(n^{1/3}(\log n)^{2/3})\). Reitwießner gives an exact depth-two threshold circuit for Andreev's function with \((5/16+o(1))n^2\) gates, settles its approximate complexity at \(\varepsilon^2 n^{3/2}\) gates up to polylogarithmic factors, and proves that sign-coherent circuits need \(\Omega(n^2/\log^3 n)\) gates.

The degree of a symmetric function

A symmetric Boolean function depends only on the Hamming weight of its input. Its exact real degree equals the degree of the univariate polynomial that interpolates its \(n+1\) values. Counting roots gives degree at least about \(n/2\) for any nonconstant function. Nisan and Szegedy asked for more.

Von zur Gathen and Roche worked modulo the largest prime \(p\) at most \(n+1\) and showed that the degree is at least \(p-1\). The deficit, \(n\) minus the degree, is therefore bounded by a prime gap. With the theorem of Baker, Harman and Pintz this gives \(O(n^{0.525})\). They conjectured that the deficit never exceeds 3, a value their two infinite families attain.

The prime route has a ceiling. Under the Riemann hypothesis it gives \(O(\sqrt{n}\log n)\), and Legendre's conjecture would give \(O(\sqrt{n})\). Prime gaps are unbounded, so no constant can come this way. Cohen, Shpilka and Tal had proposed \(n-\sqrt{n}\) as an intermediate goal.

Both papers end at the same kind of place: a clean theorem, and a precise statement of what the method cannot reach.

Sun and Wang [arXiv:2611.26446] prove a deficit of \(O(n^{1/3}(\log n)^{2/3})\). This passes the \(\sqrt{n}\) goal and beats what the Riemann hypothesis would give the old method. No prime-gap estimate is used. Every numbered result is checked in Lean 4 with Mathlib, though the authors note that the formal proofs do not always follow the written ones.

How the argument goes

A large deficit means that the values, written as signs \(\pm 1\), are orthogonal to every low-degree polynomial under alternating binomial weights. The proof uses these vanishing moments twice. First, a quantitative form of the integer Hankel determinant method from the Pólya–Carlson theorem shows that an integer sequence cannot have a deep spectral hole near \(-1\) without obeying a short linear recurrence. So on a central interval of Hamming weights the signs follow a rational recurrence of order about \(n^{2/3}\).

Second, the authors build a low-degree test polynomial that cancels the recurrence's characteristic roots near \(-1\), of which there are few, and isolates the first index where the signs leave the recurrence. That contradicts the vanishing moments. If the recurrence holds over a long stretch, height bounds force it to be periodic with bounded period, and a separate test rules this out. The exponent \(1/3\) comes from balancing the depth of the spectral hole against the size of the auxiliary polynomials.

The corollaries are concrete. A symmetric \(t\)-resilient function with \(t\) above \(Cn^{1/3}(\log n)^{2/3}\) must be parity or its negation. A balanced symmetric function other than these has a nonzero Fourier coefficient at a level no higher than the same bound.

The gap from \(\tilde O(n^{1/3})\) to 3 remains. A computer search, not part of the formalisation, confirms a deficit of at most 3 for all \(n \le 8192\). The authors observe that their proof uses the condition \(Z_k^2=1\) only on the central interval, and suggest that a stronger argument may need all of it.

Andreev's function at depth two

Andreev's function reads a bit from a table of \(N\) bits, at an address given by the parities of \(k\) blocks of input bits. Kane and Williams used it for the first superlinear gate lower bound against depth-two threshold circuits: \(\Omega(n^{3/2}/\log^3 n)\) gates. They gave an \(O(n^3/\log n)\) circuit and asked for tight bounds. For functions in \(\mathrm{P}\), nothing beyond about \(n^{3/2}\) gates is known.

Reitwießner [ECCC:TR26-330] gives an exact circuit with \((5/16+o(1))n^2\) gates and polynomial integer weights. The Fourier expansion writes the lookup as a sum of products, each a table coefficient times an address parity. A small identity turns each product into a difference of two functions of affine forms, and each of these is a staircase of threshold gates. Grouping terms by address parity leaves \(N\) terms, where Kane and Williams built each parity separately.

On the lower side, the paper proves \(\Omega(n^{3/2}/\log^2 n)\) gates and \(\Omega(n^{5/2}/\log^{5/2} n)\) wires. For agreement on a \(1/2+\varepsilon\) fraction of inputs, upper and lower bounds meet at \(\varepsilon^2 n^{3/2}\) up to polylogarithmic factors; the old lower bound had \(\varepsilon^3\). Two changes to the restriction method do the work. A surviving gate keeps its weights and only its threshold moves, so restricted circuits are cheaper to count. A union bound over all survivor counts at once replaces Markov's inequality. Every numbered result is formalised in Lean, and the paper states that it was developed with AI assistance.

Where it stops

For exact computation the gap runs from \(n^{3/2}/\log^2 n\) to \(\tfrac{5}{16}n^2\). The paper narrows where the answer can lie. Call a bottom gate sign-coherent if, inside each address block, its weights are all non-negative or all non-positive. Such circuits need \(\Omega(n^2/\log^3 n)\) gates, proved by restricting to unit cubes of block counts, along whose diagonals a sign-coherent gate changes value at most once. Both known polynomial-size circuits are sign-coherent. A circuit with \(n^{2-\delta}\) gates must therefore, for large \(n\), mix signs inside some block.

The paper also records that survivor counting cannot exceed about \(n^{3/2}/\log n\) gates, and that rectangle methods do not reach \(n^{2-o(1)}\). The worst-case lower bound improves by one logarithm; the exponent \(3/2\) for functions in \(\mathrm{P}\) is where it was.

Both papers end at the same kind of place: a clean theorem, and a precise statement of what the method cannot reach.

THE LONG VIEW

Forty years to one unique path

Hoza's unrefereed preprint [ECCC:TR26-323] gives a log-space generator with seed length \(O(\log(wn))\) that makes the lightest path unique in every width-\(w\), length-\(n\) layered digraph; with Reinhardt and Allender's algorithms this yields \(\mathrm{NL} = \mathrm{UL}\), ending a line of work that joined inductive counting (1987–88) to the Isolation Lemma (1987).

On 20 November 2026 Hoza posted “NL = UL” [ECCC:TR26-323] [S1]. It gives an explicit generator that, from a seed of \(O(\log(wn))\) bits, assigns polynomially bounded weights to the vertices of any width-\(w\), length-\(n\) layered digraph. For at least one seed, every reachable pair then has a unique lightest path. The generator runs in logarithmic space. Combined with the algorithms of Reinhardt and Allender, that is enough [S1, S2]. The parts it joins are nearly forty years old.

The question and the class

Jones showed in 1975 that directed \(s\)–\(t\) reachability is complete for \(\mathrm{NL}\) [S3]. So any question about \(\mathrm{NL}\) is a question about one graph problem. Valiant had defined \(\mathrm{UP}\), the unambiguous version of \(\mathrm{NP}\), in 1976, and \(\mathrm{UL}\) is its log-space analogue [S3, S4]. The first focused study of \(\mathrm{UL}\) and its relatives, among them FewL and the strongly unambiguous “reach” classes, was by Buntrock, Jenner, Lange and Rossmanith in 1991. Later papers credit that work and Àlvarez and Jenner (1993) together with defining the class [S3, S5, S2]. The easy chain \(\mathrm{ReachUL} \subseteq \mathrm{UL} \subseteq \mathrm{FewUL} \subseteq \mathrm{LogFew}\) was in place early [S4].

Counting, 1987–88

Immerman and Szelepcsényi proved independently that \(\mathrm{NSPACE}(s(n))\) is closed under complement for \(s(n) \ge \log n\), so \(\mathrm{NL} = \mathrm{coNL}\). They shared the 1995 Gödel Prize [S6, S3]. The method was inductive counting. A log-space machine cannot store the set \(R_k\) of vertices within \(k\) steps of \(s\). It can store \(|R_k|\), and with the correct count it can certify that \(t \notin R_k\) by guessing paths to exactly \(|R_k|\) vertices, none of them \(t\) [S6]. Borodin, Cook, Dymond, Ruzzo and Tompa reused the idea in 1989 for further complementation results, LogCFL among them [S6, S3].

Isolation, 1987

In the same period Mulmuley, U. Vazirani and V. Vazirani reduced maximum matching to inverting a single integer matrix, which gave a randomised parallel algorithm [S7, S8]. The engine was the Isolation Lemma: give the elements random weights and, with good probability, the minimum-weight solution is unique [S7, S8]. One idea counts; the other breaks ties. They were not put together for a decade.

The nonuniform theorem

Wigderson (1994) and Gál and Wigderson (1996) showed \(\mathrm{NL/poly} \subseteq \oplus\mathrm{L/poly}\). Wigderson remarked that a small change yields graphs with unique shortest paths, and wrote that he saw no application for this [S3]. Hoza calls it arguably the first sign that \(\mathrm{NL} = \mathrm{UL}\) should hold [S2].

Reinhardt and Allender found the application. Their FOCS 1997 paper, journal version SICOMP 2000, proved \(\mathrm{NL/poly} = \mathrm{UL/poly}\), with an analogous statement for LogCFL [S3, S9]. Edge weights drawn uniformly from \([1, 4n^4]\) make every minimum-weight path unique with probability at least \(3/4\), by a union bound over triples \((x, y, e)\) [S3]. The machine then tracks two numbers per level: the count \(c_k\) of vertices at distance at most \(k\), and the sum \(\Sigma_k\) of their distances. A guessed set that is too small never reaches \(c_k\). A guessed path that is too long pushes the sum past \(\Sigma_k\). If shortest paths are unique, exactly one computation survives, and if they are not, the machine notices [S3]. The advice is a good weight assignment. What was missing was a way to produce one in log space without advice.

Conditional and partial results

Allender, Reinhardt and Zhou showed in 1999 that \(\mathrm{NL} = \mathrm{UL}\) follows if deterministic linear space contains functions that need exponential-size circuits; later papers state the size bound slightly differently [S5, S4]. In 2007 Bourke, Tewari and Vinodchandran gave explicit log-space isolating weights for planar graphs, putting directed planar reachability in \(\mathrm{UL} \cap \mathrm{coUL}\) (journal version 2009). They argued that such a weighting “may not be all that difficult” for general graphs [S5, S10]. Thierauf and Wagner, and Kynčl and Vyskočil, extended this to \(K_{3,3}\)-free, \(K_5\)-free and bounded-genus graphs by reduction to the planar case [S4, S2].

Pavan, Tewari and Vinodchandran (2010, journal 2012) proved \(\mathrm{ReachFewL} \subseteq \mathrm{UL} \cap \mathrm{coUL}\). They also showed that \(\mathrm{NL} = \mathrm{UL}\) holds if and only if some polynomially bounded, \(\mathrm{UL}\)-computable weight function makes every DAG min-unique [S4]. That fixed the target.

Unconditional progress came in space. Kallampally and Tewari (2016) simulated \(\mathrm{NL}\) unambiguously in \(O(\log^2 n)\) space and polynomial time. Van Melkebeek and Prakriya (2019) brought this to \(O(\log^{3/2} n)\), using “weight assignment generators” with seed length \(O(\log(wn)\sqrt{\log n})\) [S2]. Hoza's seed drops the \(\sqrt{\log n}\), and he says he uses some of their ideas [S2]. On the conditional side, Li, Pyne and Tell (2024) weakened the 1999 hardness assumption [S2].

The matching side

The Isolation Lemma's original home moved in parallel. Fenner, Gurjar and Thierauf put bipartite perfect matching in quasi-\(\mathrm{NC}\) in 2015 by an almost complete derandomisation of isolation for that problem [S11]. Matching in general graphs followed in quasi-\(\mathrm{NC}\) [S12]. Hoza's bibliography lists a 2026 report placing bipartite matching in \(\mathrm{NC}\), and one by Applebaum showing \(\mathrm{NL} \subseteq \mathrm{Mod}_p\mathrm{L}\) for every prime \(p\) with matching techniques. Since \(\mathrm{UL} \subseteq \mathrm{Mod}_p\mathrm{L}\), the new theorem is stronger [S2].

This week

Hoza's generator gives a vertex \(v\) in layer \(i \ge 1\) the weight \(\sum_j 6^{\nu_2(i)+\ell-j} C(v)_j x_j\). Here \(C\) is a code of length \(\ell = O(\log(wn))\) and distance \(d = \Theta(\log(wn))\), \(x\) is the seed, and \(\nu_2(i)\) counts the factors of 2 in \(i\) [S2]. Every interval of layers has exactly one layer of largest \(\nu_2\), and every path must cross it. The code's distance bounds the number of seeds that create a tie at that layer. A union bound gives failure probability at most \(2^{-d} w^4 n^2\), and induction on path length finishes the proof [S2]. The machine tries seeds in turn. It uses Reinhardt and Allender's \(\mathrm{UL} \cap \mathrm{coUL}\) test to skip bad seeds, and decides reachability with the first good one [S2]. Two questions Reinhardt and Allender left open, whether \(\mathrm{UL}\) is closed under complement and whether it has complete sets, now follow from \(\mathrm{NL} = \mathrm{UL}\) and \(\mathrm{NL} = \mathrm{coNL}\) [S3].

The paper is an unrefereed preprint. Its AI disclosure says a language model was used to discover the proof and to help prepare the paper, and that the author takes responsibility for every claim [S2]. A community effort to formalise the proof reports no defect so far in the isolation argument, but other parts are still open [S13]. A proof this short is easy to check, and that is what is now being done.

This week’s result: [ECCC:TR26-323] NL = UL (William Hoza)

The technique: Min-isolating weights with double counting

Inductive counting can certify that a vertex is unreachable. It does not stop a nondeterministic machine from having many accepting guesses for a path that does exist. Reinhardt and Allender removed that choice in two steps. First, weight the graph so that every shortest path is unique. Then, at each level \(k\), track two numbers: \(c_k\), the number of vertices at distance at most \(k\), and \(\Sigma_k\), the sum of their distances.

A guess that misses a vertex falls short of \(c_k\). A guess that claims a path longer than the true distance overshoots \(\Sigma_k\). When shortest paths are unique, exactly one computation passes every check. The machine also tests uniqueness itself. If a vertex at distance \(k\) has two in-neighbours at distance \(k-1\), it raises a flag. What the method needs is a good set of weights, and Hoza's generator supplies one in log space.

Toy instance. Take the edges \(s\to a\), \(s\to b\), \(a\to t\), \(b\to t\), all of weight 1. The distances are \(s=0\), \(a=b=1\), \(t=2\). At \(k=2\), vertex \(t\) has two in-neighbours at distance 1, so the flag is raised: there are two shortest paths.

Now give \(b\to t\) weight 2, simulated by a two-edge path \(b\to m\to t\). The distances become \(s=0\), \(a=b=1\), \(m=2\), \(t=2\). The counts are \((c_0,\Sigma_0)=(1,0)\), \((c_1,\Sigma_1)=(3,2)\) and \((c_2,\Sigma_2)=(5,6)\). Since \(c_3=c_2\), the process stops. At \(k=2\), only \(a\) is an in-neighbour of \(t\) at distance 1, so no flag is raised. The path \(s\to a\to t\) is certified by a single accepting computation.

The general theorem needs weights that work for every pair at once. A random choice from \([1,4n^4]\) does so with probability at least \(3/4\). Hoza's contribution is to find such weights deterministically, by trying every one of polynomially many seeds.

Where the Hypothesis Lives

The late column. The week's papers set against each other, looking for the pattern nobody wrote down. Plain words. No favourites.

The news desk had the Riemann hypothesis and Navier–Stokes. Complexity had NL = UL. Read them for the same thing, and it is not the thing in the headlines. Read them for where each one keeps its assumptions.

Start with fluids. Alpöge and Buckmaster [News desk] claim unforced finite-time blow-up for every viscosity. Their own Lean documentation states that the top-level theorems take as a hypothesis that a self-similar profile exists. The certificate for that profile is given only as a hash. The interval-arithmetic check sits outside the formalization. A hash is a cloakroom ticket. It shows that something was handed in. It does not show you the coat. So the formal result is conditional, the repository says so, and Tao says a proper write-up remains to be done.

The Riemann story [News desk] runs the other way. The Lean and Mathlib communities certify the statement and proof of RH, a second checker found no errors in the GRH theorems, and there are about 17 million lines of it. The explanation runs past 250,000 pages and has not been released. Here the hypothesis is gone and, for now, so is the human reading. That is a different trade, not a better or worse one.

Now the week in complexity, which ran mostly on other people's theorems.

The ETH refutations credited to OpenAI and Anthropic have become an input. Firbas and Sorge [arXiv:2611.27956] prove a sparsification theorem for Cluster Editing: in \(2^{O((k\log r)/r)}\cdot n^{O(1)}\) time an instance splits into that many small weighted instances, each equivalent to a 3-SAT formula with \(2^{O(r^2\log r)}\cdot k\) clauses. Feed in the new 3-SAT algorithms and Cluster Editing falls to \(2^{o(k)}\cdot n^{O(1)}\), which is the Cao–Chen conjecture. The converse also holds, so savings flow both ways. The function that makes the exponent \(o(k)\) shrinks like \((\log\log\log k)^{3/2}/\sqrt{\log\log k}\). The conjecture is settled. No instance anyone will solve this decade notices.

Szeider [zenodo:24299623] derives what the refutations give elsewhere: every 3-CNF on \(n\) variables has a weighted model count computed by a smooth, decomposable arithmetic circuit of size \(2^{o(n)}\), hence \(2^{o(n)}\) refutations in IPS over any field of characteristic other than 2. Monotone circuits of that kind would yield DNNFs, and some 3-CNFs need DNNFs of size \(2^{\Omega(n)}\), so the small circuits must use negative constants. The detail that matters is in the changelog. Version 1 took three statements of the Anthropic preprint as assumptions. Version 2 proves them in Lean from Anthropic's public development. The hypothesis moved from the preamble into the proof. Voitle [hexagon:2611.00558v1] does the same for Subset Sum, removing the dependence on two preprints' lemmas behind the \(2^{0.4875755n}\) bound.

Molter [arXiv:2611.26080] works the opposite shift. With ETH gone, lower bounds need a new floor, and he recovers twelve deterministic running-time bounds under FPT \(\neq\) W[1], by encoding a clique choice in few bits. It is maintenance work. Someone has to do it after the foundations move.

Sun and Wang [arXiv:2611.26446] show every nonconstant symmetric Boolean function has degree at least \(n-O(n^{1/3}(\log n)^{2/3})\), with every numbered result checked in Lean. They note that the prime-gap route gives only \(\widetilde{O}(\sqrt n)\), even assuming the Riemann hypothesis. So in the week RH was certified, a paper recorded that it would not have been enough.

Then Hoza [ECCC:TR26-323]. An explicit generator with seed length \(O(\log(wn))\), computable in \(O(\log(wn))\) space, assigns polynomially bounded weights to a layered digraph so that some seed makes every reachable minimum-weight path unique. Reinhardt–Allender does the rest: NL = UL. No external algorithm, no hash, no repository caveat. The abstract calls the construction simple, and a claim like that is cheap to check.

So the drift this week is not towards any one problem. It is towards accounting. Papers increasingly state whose theorem they stand on, then spend a version discharging it in a checker. The useful question to ask of any result now is where its hypothesis lives: in the statement, in a README, in a hash, or nowhere.
