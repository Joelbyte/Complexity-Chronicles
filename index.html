FINE-GRAINED COMPLEXITY

ETH falls. Brute force keeps its job

Two analyses of the claimed October 2026 refutations of ETH find that the subexponential #3-SAT bounds certify no speed-up over brute force for any bounded-density input that could ever be written down, cut one algorithm's crossover point to about \(2^{420}\) clauses at clause density one, and observe that the Sparse Max-3-SAT Hypothesis falls too; a third paper, an AI-assisted exposition, explains how the counting procedure works.

This month two preprints, one from OpenAI and one from Anthropic, claimed deterministic algorithms that count the satisfying assignments of a 3-CNF in time \(2^{o(n)}\). If they stand, the Exponential Time Hypothesis is false. None of the papers below claims to have checked those proofs in full. They did something more useful. They got out the calculator.

The mechanism comes first. Kominers [hexagon:2610.00574v1] gives a qualitative form of the OpenAI result in an exposition that is AI-assisted and that he calls preliminary. For every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), 3-SAT on \(n\) variables and \(m\) clauses can be decided in time \(O_\varepsilon(2^{\varepsilon n}(n+m)^{O(1)})\) and polynomial space. He separates the counting procedure from the construction that supplies its input. The clause inputs are deformed so that, at designated points, the count factors into local sums. Hermite interpolation then recovers the original count. The deformations are built from coordinate swaps in a tensor basis, using a parity interpolation in the spirit of Alman and Williams.

Then the bill. Dias and Lima [arXiv:2611.00517] note that the OpenAI preprint claims \(2^{O_k(n\log\log n/\sqrt{\log n})}\) for \(k\)-CNF, and \(2^{O(n/\sqrt{\log n})}\) at bounded clause density, but states no constants. If the hidden constant \(C\) is treated as a parameter, the general bound beats \(2^n\) only beyond \(n\approx 10^{32}\) for \(C=2\) and \(10^{3517}\) for \(C=10\). The constants they extract from the tensor-contraction proof are worse. At bounded density the certified running time stays above \(2^n\) unless \(\log_2(n+m)>10^9\). Their conclusion is that the bounds are subexponential as \(n\to\infty\), but certify no speed-up over brute force for any bounded-density input that could ever be written down. They also prove limits for the clause-product form of the interpolation scheme. In a cost model matching the preprint's evaluation, it costs \(2^{\Omega(\sqrt{\mathrm{tw}})}\) at treewidth \(\mathrm{tw}\), and \(2^{\Omega(n^{1/3})}\) even on path formulas that are trivially countable. They explain why the preprint's sharper routes escape these limits.

Majdoddin [arXiv:2610.41622] makes the Anthropic #3-SAT recursion explicit and improves it by proved changes. At clause density one, the crossover point drops from about \(2^{33858}\) clauses to about \(2^{420}\). For general 3-SAT, his estimate in the number of variables drops from \(2^{2.2\cdot 10^{12}}\) to \(2^{8.6\cdot 10^9}\). That is progress, of a kind. With integer tables, the recursion counts the assignments satisfying exactly \(j\) clauses, for every \(j\), in time \(2^{O(m/\sqrt{\log m})}\). He observes that this refutes the Sparse Max-3-SAT Hypothesis of Alman and Vassilevska Williams. The same refutation also follows from either ETH refutation via the reduction of Impagliazzo, Paturi and Zane.

None of this gives a practical algorithm, and the gap will not close by tinkering. Majdoddin defines a rewrite-and-merge class containing the recursion and all his constructions. He proves that, in his main-term model, no grid algorithm in that class can gain for general 3-SAT below about \(2^{6000}\) variables. For two balanced homogeneous merges, he also excludes running times of the form \(2^{n^{1-c}}\). The hypothesis may be dead. Brute force has not been told.

MATRIX MULTIPLICATION

Omega drops to nine-quarters

Shpilka gives an elementary, self-contained proof of the bound \(\omega \le 9/4\) claimed in an OpenAI manuscript, with explicit algorithms of exponent below \(9/4+\varepsilon\) and base matrix size at most \(2^{(1/\varepsilon)^{O(1)}}\) for every \(\varepsilon>0\).

A recent OpenAI manuscript proves that the exponent of matrix multiplication satisfies \(\omega \le 9/4\). The previous record stood a little above 2.37. Shpilka [ECCC:TR26-281] now gives an elementary, self-contained proof. He follows the original ideas but implements them differently, so that the algorithms are explicit. For every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is an algorithm with exponent below \(9/4+\varepsilon\) and base matrix size at most \(2^{(1/\varepsilon)^{O(1)}}\).

The base size is the small print. Nobody will be multiplying matrices this way before lunch. But an independent, elementary route to the bound is exactly what a claim of this size needs. An appendix describes the original proof, which goes through Strassen's theory of the asymptotic spectrum.

Similar techniques reach rectangular multiplication. Starting from Strassen's tensor, Shpilka gives elementary proofs that \(\omega(1,\frac{2}{3\log 3},1)=2\) and \(\omega(1,0.539,2)=3\). Stronger rectangular bounds are already known; these proofs are included for their simplicity. The longer history is in this week's perspective.

PROOF COMPLEXITY

Sherali-Adams loses twice

Res(2) is exponentially stronger than Sherali-Adams, and Sherali-Adams is not size-automatable unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\); both results come from a new polynomial-interpolation method for Sherali-Adams degree lower bounds.

Acheampong, Davis and Robere [ECCC:TR26-288] resolve two open problems about the Sherali-Adams proof system. First, Res(2), which manipulates 2-DNF formulas, is exponentially stronger than Sherali-Adams. Sherali-Adams polynomially simulates Resolution, so the separation is sharp. One step up the bounded-depth Frege hierarchy, Sherali-Adams is already outclassed.

Second, Sherali-Adams is not size-automatable unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). Short proofs may exist. Finding them in time polynomial in their size is, under the usual assumption, off the table. Many researchers have asked about the automatability of weak proof systems, and this is one of the answers.

Both results rest on one new technique: lower bounds on Sherali-Adams degree via polynomial interpolation. A technique that pays twice in one paper is worth watching.

[ECCC:TR26-288] Polynomial Interpolation for Sherali-Adams Lower Bounds (Nathan Acheampong, Ben Davis, Robert Robere)

COMMUNICATION COMPLEXITY

Sign rank breaks under AND

An explicit matrix of sign rank \(\Theta(d)\) whose conjunction with itself has sign rank \(2^{\Theta(d)}\) shows that the unbounded-error communication class \(\mathrm{UPP}^{\mathrm{cc}}\) is not closed under intersection.

Bun and Juvekar [ECCC:TR26-289] (also [arXiv:2610.37634]) work with sign rank: the minimum rank of a real matrix that agrees with a given \(\pm 1\) matrix entrywise in sign. For every sufficiently large \(d\), they construct an explicit \(\pm 1\) matrix \(A\) with \(2^{\Theta(d^2)}\) rows and columns and sign rank \(\Theta(d)\). Its conjunction \(A \land A\) has sign rank \(2^{\Theta(d)}\). Here \(A \land A\) is the problem of evaluating \(A\) on two disjoint inputs and taking the AND of the results.

The logarithm of sign rank characterises unbounded-error communication complexity. So a problem costing about \(\log d\) bits becomes one costing about \(d\) bits when two instances are joined by an AND. That proves the title: \(\mathrm{UPP}^{\mathrm{cc}}\) is not closed under intersection. One small conjunction produces one exponential jump.

Promise CSP

Two colours promised, a few logs delivered

Bhangale, KM and Rubin-Toles prove it is quasi-NP-hard to colour a 2-colourable 3-uniform hypergraph on \(n\) vertices with \(O((\log n)^{1/8})\) colours, an exponential improvement on the \(O((\log\log n)^{1/3})\) of Dinur, Regev and Smyth from 2005. Separately, Brakensiek, Conlon, Guruswami, He, Nie, Sankar and Yu use limits on 2-query locally decodable and correctable codes to bound CSP non-redundancy, including the first Boolean predicate whose non-redundancy is provably not of the form \(n^{k\pm o(1)}\).

Some problems do not move for twenty years. Then they move by an exponential, and everyone pretends they expected it.

This week it was hypergraph colouring. The question is simple to state. You are handed a 3-uniform hypergraph and a promise that it can be properly coloured with two colours, meaning no edge is monochromatic. You are not asked to find the two-colouring. You are asked for something far more modest: a proper colouring with some larger number of colours. How many colours can you be forced to use before the problem becomes hard?

What was known

In the language this paper's reader speaks, this is a promise CSP. The yes-instances satisfy a strong template and the algorithm need only satisfy a weak one. The programme to classify such problems has made real progress, but approximate colouring has stayed stubborn, and the hypergraph case is the one where hardness has historically been easiest to get and slowest to improve.

Between a power of \(\log n\) and a power of \(n\) there is a great deal of room, and nobody knows where the truth lives.

The reference point was Dinur, Regev and Smyth, published in Combinatorica in 2005. They showed it is quasi-NP-hard to colour a 2-colourable 3-uniform hypergraph with \(O((\log\log n)^{1/3})\) colours. That is a function that grows, technically. It is not a function anyone would want to plot.

What is new

Bhangale, KM and Rubin-Toles [arXiv:2610.37869] raise the bar to \(O((\log n)^{1/8})\) colours, still under quasi-NP-hardness. The exponent \(1/8\) looks modest. The change from \(\log\log n\) to \(\log n\) under the root is not. It is an exponential improvement on a bound that had stood for two decades, and the authors say as much in plain terms.

The hedging matters and should be stated plainly. Quasi-NP-hardness means that a polynomial-time algorithm would put \(\mathrm{NP}\) inside quasi-polynomial time. It is not \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness under polynomial-time reductions. The paper is twenty pages and the abstract does not describe the construction, so this newspaper will not describe it either. The tags it carries, PCP among them, suggest the usual machinery of label cover and long-code-style gadgets, but suggestion is all it is.

Why the reader should care

The gap here is the whole story. Hardness has crept up from \(\log\log\) territory to a fractional power of \(\log n\). Efficient colouring algorithms for this promise, by the general understanding of the area, need a number of colours that grows as a power of \(n\). Between a power of \(\log n\) and a power of \(n\) there is a great deal of room, and nobody knows where the truth lives. Every step that pushes hardness upward narrows the space in which an algebraic or SDP-based algorithm might be hiding, and tells the classification programme which minions it should expect to be hard.

The other end: how much of an instance matters

The second paper asks a different question about CSPs, but one with consequences for anyone who cares about kernels and sparsifiers. Brakensiek, Conlon, Guruswami, He, Nie, Sankar and Yu [arXiv:2610.38664] study non-redundancy: the maximum number of clauses in an instance of a CSP over a fixed relation that can be logically independent, so that no clause is implied by the others. The abstract notes that this quantity has turned up in query complexity, sparsification, kernelization and streaming. Put crudely, it caps how far one can compress an instance without losing information.

The new idea is to borrow from coding theory. Known structural limits on 2-query locally decodable codes and locally correctable codes are translated into limits on the size of certain non-redundant CSP instances. A large non-redundant instance would, in effect, give a better local code than is known to exist. This builds on earlier work of Brakensiek and Guruswami at STOC 2025, which bounded non-redundancy using matching vector codes.

Two outcomes are reported. First, improved upper bounds on the non-redundancy of the BCK predicate introduced by Bessiere, Carbonnel and Katsirelos at AAAI 2020, and of its well-studied Boolean variant. The abstract does not give the numbers and neither shall we. Second, and more curious, the first Boolean predicate whose non-redundancy is provably not of the form \(n^{k\pm o(1)}\) for any positive integer \(k\). The tidy expectation that these quantities come in integer exponents is now, for at least one predicate, provably wrong.

What links them

Both papers are about the fine structure of CSPs below the level at which the decision dichotomy is settled. The Bulatov and Zhuk theorem tells us which finite-domain CSPs are in \(\mathrm{P}\). It does not tell us how many colours a promise buys, or how small an instance can be made before it stops meaning the same thing. Those are the questions that keep the field employed.

What remains open

For hypergraph colouring: whether hardness can be pushed to a power of \(n\), whether quasi-NP-hardness can be upgraded to \(\mathrm{NP}\)-hardness at this range, and whether the exponent \(1/8\) is an artefact of the method. For non-redundancy: what the true exponents for the BCK predicates are, and how strange non-redundancy can get once integer exponents are off the table. The code-theoretic route gives upper bounds; matching lower bounds are another matter.

Twenty years for one exponential. The field may hope the next one comes quicker. It usually does not.

Proof complexity

Sherali-Adams meets its betters

Acheampong, Davis and Robere prove that Res(2) is exponentially stronger than Sherali-Adams, and that Sherali-Adams is not size-automatable unless P = NP. Part proves \(2^{\Omega(\epsilon n)}\) depth-three lower bounds for Tseitin formulas in Hrubeš' Algebraic Monotone Calculus when \(\epsilon=\omega(\log n/n)\). Conneryd proves near-\(n/2\) polynomial calculus degree bounds for random \(O(\log n)\)-CNFs over every field, which give superpolynomial depth-2 \(\mathrm{AC}^0[p]\)-Frege lower bounds via Pang's tradeoff.

Proof complexity has a habit of leaving its weakest systems half-mapped. Everybody knows roughly where they sit. Nobody has the theorem. This week three papers fill in more of the chart around Sherali-Adams, the linear-programming hierarchy that constraint people meet early and never quite shake off. Two of the three pieces of news are things the field had been asking for by name.

Two answers from one technique

The headline paper is by Acheampong, Davis and Robere [ECCC:TR26-288]. It settles two open problems with a single new tool.

The first is a separation. Sherali-Adams polynomially simulates Resolution; that much was known, and the abstract states it. Res(2) is Resolution allowed to work with 2-DNF formulas rather than plain clauses, which makes it the first rung above Resolution on the bounded-depth Frege ladder. The authors show that Res(2) is exponentially stronger than Sherali-Adams. So Sherali-Adams sits above Resolution and is beaten outright by the next system up. The abstract calls this a sharp separation between Sherali-Adams and the bounded-depth Frege hierarchy. That is fair. There is not much room left between the two.

It is rare for a paper to tell you exactly where its method stops. This one does.

The second result is about automatability: whether proofs can be found in time polynomial in the size of the shortest proof. The authors prove that Sherali-Adams is not size-automatable unless \(\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}\). The abstract notes that this had been asked by many researchers. It joins the list of weak systems where short proofs exist and finding them is as hard as anything in \(\mathrm{NP}\).

Both results come from a new way of proving lower bounds on Sherali-Adams degree, via polynomial interpolation. The abstract gives no further detail of the mechanics, and this paper will not supply any. What can be said is that one degree technique delivered both a separation and a hardness-of-search result, which is a decent return on a single idea.

Monotone circuits as proofs

Part's paper [ECCC:TR26-305], also on arXiv as [arXiv:2611.01055], works in a stranger corner. In Hrubeš' Algebraic Monotone Calculus a DNF tautology \(A\) is proved by a monotone arithmetic circuit computing \(\chi(A)-\epsilon\cdot\chi(1)\), where the coefficient of \(\chi(A)\) at an assignment counts the terms of \(A\) it satisfies. Proof size is circuit size. The shift \(\epsilon>0\) is existentially quantified, so a lower bound has to survive arbitrarily small \(\epsilon\). That is the catch, and Part spends most of the paper on it.

At depth three a proof is a nonnegative tensor decomposition. Restrict the summands to subcube indicators and you get conical juntas, a system the paper shows is polynomially equivalent to Sherali-Adams when size counts conjunctions over the twin variables. Allow arbitrary nonnegative rank-one summands and you get something stronger. For that general fragment Part adapts Rothvoß' hyperplane separation method to monotone tensor rank and proves that Tseitin formulas on constant-degree expanders of large girth need size \(2^{\Omega(\epsilon n)}\) whenever \(\epsilon=\omega(\log n/n)\).

The bound cannot reach every \(\epsilon\), and the paper says why. At shift \(2^{-\Theta(n\log n)}\) Tseitin acquires polynomial-size depth-three proofs. That fact doubles as an exponential separation between the general depth-three fragment and the Sherali-Adams-like subcube fragment. To cover every \(\epsilon\ge 2^{-\Omega(n)}\), Part composes Tseitin with a constant-size index gadget, turning a Sherali-Adams degree bound into a depth-three size bound. Depth three is essential: Tseitin has monotone proofs of size \(O(n^3)\) and depth \(O(\log n)\) at every \(\epsilon\).

Beyond depth three Part bounds the nonnegative rank of a flattening by \(L^{2^t}\) for a monotone formula with \(L\) leaves and mixed-product depth \(t\). The abstract is candid that this is empty for Tseitin. Instead it is aimed elsewhere: approximate extension complexity gives bounds for perfect matching and odd factor instances, and linear programming integrality gaps for planted constraint satisfaction give an explicit family of planted Tseitin DNFs hard at inverse-polynomial shift. CSP readers will note where the hard instances came from.

Random formulas, modular gates

Conneryd [arXiv:2610.40397] goes after random \(k\)-CNFs with \(M=n2^k\) clauses, for \(\omega_n(1)\le k\le O(\log n)\). With high probability, refuting them needs polynomial calculus degree at least \(n/2-O(n/\sqrt{k})\), over every field. Feed that into Pang's recent tradeoff between \(\mathrm{AC}^0[p]\)-Frege size and polynomial calculus degree, and you get a size lower bound of \(\exp(\Omega_p(\sqrt{k}\log n))\) for depth-2 \(\mathrm{AC}^0[p]\)-Frege. In this range the bound is superpolynomial in \(M\) while \(M\) stays polynomial in \(n\).

The paper then closes its own door. A nearly matching upper bound of \(n/2-n\ln(k)/2k+O(n/k)\) on the degree shows that random CNFs will not carry Pang's tradeoff to full \(\mathrm{AC}^0[p]\)-Frege. Other formula families are needed. It is rare for a paper to tell you exactly where its method stops. This one does.

What is left

The chart is clearer but not finished. The interpolation technique for Sherali-Adams degree is new, and the obvious question is how far up it travels; the abstract claims nothing beyond Sherali-Adams, so neither will this paper. Part's lower bounds stop at depth three for Tseitin, and the general depth-three bound stops short of every \(\epsilon\) for a reason that is a theorem rather than a gap in effort. Conneryd has shown that full \(\mathrm{AC}^0[p]\)-Frege via Pang's route needs formulas other than random ones, and has not said which.

That is three careful papers and three honest stopping points. In this trade, that is a good week.

Fine-grained complexity

Inside the counting engine

Two AI-assisted expositions, by Kominers and O'Donnell, describe how OpenAI's claimed refutation of the Exponential Time Hypothesis is supposed to decide 3-SAT in time \(O_\varepsilon(2^{\varepsilon n}(n+m)^{O(1)})\) and polynomial space for every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\). Neither is a verification. Separately, Kozma proves unconditionally that #3SAT in time \(2^{O(n/f(n))}\) would give #PPM in time \(2^{O(n\sqrt{\log f(n)/f(n)})}\).

The claim is simple to state and hard to swallow. 3-SAT, the problem a generation of conditional lower bounds has leaned on, would run in time \(2^{\varepsilon n}\) for every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\). That is the end of the Exponential Time Hypothesis. Since SETH implies ETH, it is the end of SETH as well.

The original manuscript is not on this desk. What we have are two expositions, both written with the help of language models, and both labelled accordingly. This feature sets out what they say the machine does. It does not say the machine works.

What the expositions claim

Kominers [hexagon:2610.00574v1] gives what he calls a preliminary exposition of a qualitative form of the main result: for every fixed \(\varepsilon>0\), 3-SAT on \(n\) variables and \(m\) clauses can be decided in time \(O_\varepsilon(2^{\varepsilon n}(n+m)^{O(1)})\) and in polynomial space. The space bound deserves a second look. Plenty of exponential-time methods buy speed with memory. This one, as described, does not.

Parity is the function that famously refuses to be approximated by low-degree polynomials; here an approximating polynomial for parity is apparently doing the work in an upper bound.

The algorithm counts. It computes the number of satisfying assignments, and the decision problem falls out. That is the usual direction of travel for algebraic methods, and the reason the downstream papers this week are about counting problems.

The paper is tagged AI-assisted, with ChatGPT, Claude Opus and Poe listed as tools; its provenance is recorded as mixed, and human understanding as “some”. The O'Donnell exposition [hexagon:2610.00572v1] says it was produced through discussion with an LLM. Its record lists provenance as “no-human-author-claimed” and human understanding as “none”. A reader should weigh it accordingly. A famous name on the header is not the same thing as a checked proof, and the document does not pretend otherwise.

How the engine is meant to run

Kominers separates two things that the original apparently bundles: a general counting procedure, and a construction that feeds it.

The procedure works by deformation. The clause inputs are perturbed so that, at certain designated points, the count of satisfying assignments factors into local sums. Local sums are cheap. The original count is then recovered by Hermite interpolation, the classical technique that rebuilds a polynomial from its values and derivatives at a set of points. The idea, as far as the abstract goes, is to evaluate a hard global quantity only where it happens to be easy, and to let interpolation carry the answer back to where it is hard.

The construction supplies the deformations. According to Kominers, they are built from coordinate swaps in a tensor basis, and lean on a parity interpolation “in the spirit of Alman and Williams”. O'Donnell's exposition puts the same ingredient at the centre: it highlights the approximating polynomial for parity, and writes its matrix version in a light dose of quantum notation.

Readers raised on circuit lower bounds will notice the irony. Parity is the function that famously refuses to be approximated by low-degree polynomials; that refusal is the engine of a good part of the lower-bound literature. Here an approximating polynomial for parity is apparently doing the work in an upper bound. The abstracts do not say in what sense the approximation holds, over which ring, or at what degree. Those are exactly the questions a referee would ask first.

What the abstracts also do not give is constants. The statement is \(O_\varepsilon\), and the subscript is where the bodies are buried. A \(2^{\varepsilon n}\) algorithm with a hidden factor that is astronomical in \(1/\varepsilon\) refutes a hypothesis without troubling a SAT solver. That is no objection to the mathematics. ETH is an asymptotic statement and an asymptotic refutation counts. It is an objection to anyone who promises faster verification software by Christmas.

The first casualty: pattern matching

Kozma's paper [arXiv:2610.41048] is the sort of thing that appears when a field takes a claim seriously enough to plan for it. It concerns #PPM: count the occurrences of a permutation pattern in a host permutation of length \(n\). The best known algorithm, for both the decision and counting versions, runs in \(O(1.415^n)\), due to Gawrychowski and Rzepecki. In the other direction, Jelínek, Opler and Pekárek showed PPM is ETH-hard: a \(2^{o(n/\log n)}\) algorithm would give subexponential 3SAT.

Kozma supplies the converse. If #3SAT runs in time \(2^{O(n/f(n))}\), then #PPM runs in time \(2^{O(n\sqrt{\log f(n)/f(n)})}\). Feed in any unbounded \(f\) and #PPM becomes subexponential. The square root costs something, but the direction is the point: the exponential complexities of the two problems are now tied in both directions, up to those losses.

The reduction is unconditional. It stands whether or not the counting engine does. If the claim collapses, Kozma has still pinned #PPM to #3SAT more tightly than before. If the claim holds, it is an early entry in what would become a very long list.

What remains open

For this reader the consequences are close to home. A great deal of fine-grained and parameterized work on CSPs is stated relative to ETH or SETH; those lower bounds would lose their hypothesis, though not their reductions. The reductions would turn into algorithms.

But the order of business is plain. First, a human-checked proof. Second, an account of the parity polynomial precise enough to see why it does not contradict what we know about parity. Third, constants. Two expositions, one of them with no claimed human understanding, are a start. They are not a verdict. This paper will report the verdict when there is one, and not before.

THE LONG VIEW

Fifty-seven years to nine-quarters

Shpilka's elementary proof of OpenAI's claimed bound [ECCC:TR26-281] gives, for every \(\varepsilon>0\), an explicit matrix multiplication algorithm with exponent below \(9/4+\varepsilon\), an unrefereed result that caps fifty-seven years of chasing \(\omega\) down from Strassen's \(2.81\) to the laser-method decimals near \(2.371\).

Seven for eight

Until the late 1960s the working belief was that \(\omega=3\) [S1]. Strassen's 1969 note, “Gaussian elimination is not optimal”, ended that belief in three pages [S2]. Two \(2\times 2\) matrices can be multiplied with seven multiplications instead of eight. The formulas never use commutativity, so the entries may themselves be blocks. The recursion \(T(n)=7T(n/2)+O(n^2)\) then gives \(O(n^{\log_2 7})=O(n^{2.81})\) [S3]. The same paper showed that inversion, the determinant and Gaussian elimination cost no more than multiplication, up to constants [S3]. One saved product, and a field had its exponent.

The next decade was a hunt for small exact schemes. Pan's 1978 construction multiplied \(70\times 70\) matrices with 143,640 products, for \(\omega<2.796\) [S1]. Honest work, bought one multiplication at a time.

Approximation is free

In 1979 Bini, Capovani, Romani and Lotti let the coefficients depend on an indeterminate \(\lambda\). They computed the product only up to terms that vanish as \(\lambda\to 0\) [S4]. Ten products sufficed for a small rectangular product. Three cyclically permuted copies gave \(12\times 12\) multiplication with 1000 products, so \(\omega\le\log_{12}1000<2.78\) [S1]. The idea is now called border rank. Bini showed that such approximate algorithms bound \(\omega\) just as exact ones do [S1].

Schönhage's 1981 paper on partial and total matrix multiplication [S5] added the asymptotic sum inequality, the \(\tau\)-theorem. A direct sum of several smaller rectangular products, computed together, is enough to bound \(\omega\) [S6]. Formally, if the border rank of \(\bigoplus_i\langle a_i,b_i,c_i\rangle\) is at most \(r\), then \(\omega\le 3\tau\), where \(\sum_i(a_ib_ic_i)^\tau=r\) [S7].

His example made the point. A \(3\times1\) by \(1\times3\) product and a \(1\times4\) by \(4\times1\) product can be computed together, approximately, with 10 products. Any exact method needs at least 13 [S1]. Border rank is not additive. The bound came to \(\omega<2.548\) [S6], and he showed that \(\omega\) depends only on the characteristic of the field [S7].

Coppersmith and Winograd then proved that \(\omega\) is a limit point, attained by no single algorithm [S8]. That is why everyone writes \(O(n^{\omega+\epsilon})\) [S3].

The laser

Strassen's laser method of 1986–87 changed the starting material. Begin with a tensor that is not a matrix product at all [S1]. Take a large tensor power and zero out parts until many independent matrix products remain. Then feed them to the \(\tau\)-theorem [S1, S6]. It gave \(\omega<2.48\) [S9]. By 2020 every record set since 1986 had used it [S7].

In the same years Strassen built the theory of the asymptotic spectrum of tensors, published in Crelle in 1988 [S10]. Tensors form a semiring under direct sum and tensor product. Spectral points are maps to the non-negative reals that are monotone under restriction, normalised on diagonal tensors, additive and multiplicative. A duality theorem describes asymptotic rank through them [S11]. Bounding the asymptotic rank of matrix-multiplication tensors bounds \(\omega\) [S12]. Keep this in mind. It returns.

Coppersmith and Winograd sharpened the laser with the Salem–Spencer theorem on dense sets of integers with no three-term arithmetic progression [S13, S14]. They reached \(\omega<2.388\) in 1987 and \(2.376\) in 1990, the latter from the second tensor power [S15, S16]. That number stood until 2010 [S3].

The decimal era

Stothers broke the silence in 2010 with the fourth tensor power; the third had given nothing [S1]. The published Davie–Stothers bound is \(\omega<2.37369\) [S17]. In 2012 Vassilevska Williams turned the analysis of higher powers into a computer-solved optimisation problem, for \(\omega<2.3727\) [S18, S17]. Le Gall analysed further powers and reached \(2.3728639\) in 2014 [S19].

Then the bill arrived. Ambainis, Filmus and Le Gall showed in 2014 that analysing ever higher powers of the Coppersmith–Winograd identity cannot give \(\omega<2.3725\). A wide class of variants cannot go below \(2.3078\), and so cannot reach 2 [S20]. Alman and Vassilevska Williams, and separately Christandl, Vrana and Zuiddam, extended such barriers to major generalisations of the known approaches [S21].

The records kept coming anyway, at roughly one decimal place per conference cycle:

  • Alman and Vassilevska Williams' refined laser method gave \(2.3728596\) [S9].
  • Duan, Wu and Zhou found a hidden combination loss and compensated with asymmetric hashing. They got \(2.371866\) [S9], below the 2.3725 barrier [S3].
  • Vassilevska Williams, Xu, Xu and Zhou reached \(2.371552\) [S9].
  • Alman, Duan, Vassilevska Williams, Xu, Xu and Zhou reached \(2.371339\) [S22].
  • In August 2026 Dupont and colleagues, using numerical optimisation and AlphaEvolve, reached \(2.371177\) [S23].

Fifty-seven years had moved the exponent from 2.81 to 2.371.

The rectangular story ran in parallel. The dual exponent \(\alpha\) is the largest \(k\) with \(\omega(1,k,1)=2\). Coppersmith put it at \(0.172\) in 1982 [S16]. Vassilevska Williams, Xu, Xu and Zhou brought it to \(0.321334\) [S24]. Methods built on the big Coppersmith–Winograd tensors cannot push it past \(0.6218\) [S25].

Nine-quarters

On 24 September 2026 OpenAI released a manuscript claiming \(\omega\) below 2.258. It was one of 722 manuscripts the company says an unreleased internal model produced [S26]. A second manuscript, dated 2 October, is titled “An Upper Bound of 9/4 for the Matrix Multiplication Exponent” and claims \(\omega\le 9/4\) over \(\mathbb{C}\) [S27, S28].

The route bounds matrix multiplication via polynomial multiplication [S26]. It works inside Strassen's asymptotic-spectrum framework, with non-constructive arguments [S28]. A Lean formalisation is reported [S26, S27]. OpenAI itself says its batch sits at different stages of verification [S26].

Shpilka's note [ECCC:TR26-281] prompts this look back [S29]. It gives an elementary, self-contained proof. It follows the original ideas but implements them so as to produce explicit algorithms: for every \(\varepsilon>0\), an exponent below \(9/4+\varepsilon\) with base matrix size at most \(2^{(1/\varepsilon)^{O(1)}}\). Starting from Strassen's tensor, it also proves \(\omega(1,\frac{2}{3\log 3},1)=2\) and \(\omega(1,0.539,2)=3\), while noting that stronger rectangular bounds are known [S29].

There are other accounts. Pass gives a constructive one [S28]. Amanov and co-authors extend the bound to every field and prove barriers [S30]. Li identifies an untwisting lemma for semidirect sums as the core of the proof [S31]. None of this is peer review. Four independent re-derivations is still more than most claims get.

One inference of our own. \(9/4\) sits below the 2.3078 barrier. Whatever the new proof is, it is not the laser method applied to Coppersmith–Winograd powers in the form Ambainis, Filmus and Le Gall ruled out.

What it does not show

It does not show \(\omega=2\) [S3]. Lower bounds stay where they were: Raz's \(\Omega(n^2\log n)\), and that only for bounded-coefficient circuits [S3]. A base matrix of size \(2^{(1/\varepsilon)^{O(1)}}\) is not a library routine. Like every record of recent decades, it is galactic [S3]. Strassen's seven products, meanwhile, pay their way for \(n\) above roughly 100 and sit in BLAS-type libraries [S3]. Fifty-seven years on, the 1969 algorithm is still the one doing the work.

The technique: Rank plus recursion: Strassen's seven products

A bilinear algorithm for \(k\times k\) matrices forms \(r\) products. Each product is a linear form in the entries of \(A\) times a linear form in the entries of \(B\). Every entry of \(C=AB\) is then a linear combination of these products. No step uses commutativity, so the entries may be matrix blocks. Applied recursively to blocks, the algorithm costs \(T(n)=r T(n/k)+O(n^2)\), so \(\omega\le\log_k r\). In tensor language, \(r\) is the rank of the matrix-multiplication tensor \(\langle k,k,k\rangle\). Border rank and Schönhage's \(\tau\)-theorem extend the same accounting to approximate algorithms and to direct sums.

Strassen's case is \(k=2\), \(r=7\). The seven products are:

  • \(M_1=(a_{11}+a_{22})(b_{11}+b_{22})\)
  • \(M_2=(a_{21}+a_{22})b_{11}\)
  • \(M_3=a_{11}(b_{12}-b_{22})\)
  • \(M_4=a_{22}(b_{21}-b_{11})\)
  • \(M_5=(a_{11}+a_{12})b_{22}\)
  • \(M_6=(a_{21}-a_{11})(b_{11}+b_{12})\)
  • \(M_7=(a_{12}-a_{22})(b_{21}+b_{22})\)

The entries of \(C\) are:

  • \(C_{11}=M_1+M_4-M_5+M_7\)
  • \(C_{12}=M_3+M_5\)
  • \(C_{21}=M_2+M_4\)
  • \(C_{22}=M_1-M_2+M_3+M_6\)

Toy instance. Take \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}\) and \(B=\begin{pmatrix}5&6\\7&8\end{pmatrix}\). The products are \(M_1,\dots,M_7=65,35,-2,8,24,22,-30\). Then:

  • \(C_{11}=65+8-24-30=19\)
  • \(C_{12}=-2+24=22\)
  • \(C_{21}=35+8=43\)
  • \(C_{22}=65-35-2+22=50\)

That is exactly \(AB\).

At size \(n=2^k\) the recursion uses \(7^k\) multiplications instead of \(8^k\): 16,807 against 32,768 at \(n=32\). Asymptotically this gives \(\omega\le\log_2 7\approx 2.807\).

Every later bound, up to the claimed \(9/4\), is this same arithmetic: find a structure whose (border, or asymptotic) rank is small relative to the matrix products it contains, then recurse. The difficulty lies entirely in finding the structure.

One Toolkit, Two Targets

Thirty years on the complexity beat. Has seen every reduction twice and trusts none of them. Reads the week's abstracts after midnight, looking for the pattern the authors did not notice, and usually finds it.

Case file, week ending 1 November. Two manuscripts, filed independently, both claiming the Exponential Time Hypothesis is false. One is credited to OpenAI. The other is credited to Anthropic [hexagon:2610.00577v1]. It has a two-sentence abstract and a claimed Lean 4 formalisation. Within days the desk had a dozen papers downstream. That is not a theme. That is a weather system.

Sort the downstream by type.

Expositions. Kominers [hexagon:2610.00574v1] separates the counting procedure from its input. The clause inputs are deformed so that the count factors into local sums at designated points. Hermite interpolation then recovers the count, with a parity interpolation in the spirit of Alman and Williams. O'Donnell [hexagon:2610.00572v1] foregrounds the approximating polynomial for parity, in light quantum language. Its provenance tag reads human understanding: none, which is at least candid.

Corollaries. Kozma [arXiv:2610.41048] reverses a known reduction, so faster #3SAT gives faster #PPM. Bhattacharjee [hexagon:2610.00594v1] extends the counting to weighted sums and Max-\(k\)-SAT. Tulsyan [hexagon:2610.00672v1] counts Hamiltonian cycles in sparse graphs, conditional on three internal results. Rudich and Rousseau [hexagon:2610.00600v1] offer deterministic factoring in \(N^{o(1)}\), backed by a 13-line Lean statement. Each stands or falls with the engine.

Audits. Dias and Lima [arXiv:2611.00517] extract constants from the tensor-contraction proof. At bounded density, the certified running time stays above \(2^n\) unless \(\log_2(n+m)>10^9\). Majdoddin [arXiv:2610.41622] makes Anthropic's recursion explicit. He lowers the crossover to about \(2^{420}\) clauses at density one. He also proves that no grid algorithm in his rewrite-and-merge class gains for general 3-SAT below about \(2^{6000}\) variables. In passing, he notes the Sparse Max-3-SAT Hypothesis falls too. The hypothesis has been refuted for every input too large to exist. Asymptotics has always been generous that way.

Now the pattern. Read the ETH papers alongside Shpilka's exposition of OpenAI's \(\omega\le 9/4\) [ECCC:TR26-281]. His appendix describes the original proof, which goes through Strassen's asymptotic spectrum. Majdoddin proves his limits with quantum functionals. His Signed Network Sums model contracts the four-node complete graph in \(N^{23/6}\) if \(\omega=2\), and in about \(N^{3.93}\) on the current preprint curve. The headline bound Dias and Lima audit is proved by tensor contraction. Stein Naves de Brito [hexagon:2610.00692v1] shows by a rank floor that the gain parameter \(\rho=1/\sqrt2\) is optimal for pieces of that shape. The SAT claim and the matrix-multiplication claim are not two stories. They are one toolkit, tensors and interpolation, aimed at two targets in the same month.

The toolkit is also working the other side of the street. Acheampong, Davis and Robere [ECCC:TR26-288] prove Sherali-Adams degree lower bounds by polynomial interpolation. They show Res(2) is exponentially stronger than Sherali-Adams, and that Sherali-Adams is not size-automatable unless P = NP. Part [ECCC:TR26-305] bounds depth-three Algebraic Monotone Calculus proofs by monotone tensor rank. Garlik, Gryaznov, Lu, Santhanam and Tzameret [ECCC:TR26-277] formalise rank-method circuit lower bounds in \(VNC^2\). Under an algebraic Krajíček–Razborov analogue, they suggest that general circuits will need stronger reasoning. So the same week has interpolation for algorithms, interpolation for lower bounds, and rank for both.

My reading. Attention has moved from proving to auditing, and the audits are written in the language of the thing audited. The constants have been extracted, and the gap is large enough that Majdoddin calls it structural. If limits on these methods come, they will likely look like the rank floor Stein Naves de Brito computed. Watch the question he leaves open, whether \(\rho\ge 1/2\).

Meanwhile, with nobody looking, Bun and Juvekar [arXiv:2610.37634] showed \(\mathrm{UPP}^{\mathrm{cc}}\) is not closed under intersection. Bhangale, KM and Rubin-Toles [arXiv:2610.37869] exponentially improved the hardness of colouring 2-colourable 3-uniform hypergraphs. An ordinary week would have led with either. Nothing above is verified. Everything is filed.
