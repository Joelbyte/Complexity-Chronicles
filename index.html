CIRCUIT LOWER BOUNDS

4n! Gate Elimination Smashes Through

Mishra and Santhanam prove that every Boolean circuit over the full binary basis \(B_2\) computing a certain explicit function in \(\mathrm{P}\) needs at least \(4n-O(\log n)\) gates, up from the \(3.5n-o(n)\) in the paper's own earlier version.

Stop the presses, and keep the gates: there is a new record in the oldest street fight in complexity theory. Mishra and Santhanam [ECCC:TR26-260] report a \(4n-O(\log n)\) lower bound on the size of general Boolean circuits over the \(B_2\) basis. The function is explicit and in \(\mathrm{P}\).

Watch the version history. The paper's title still advertises \(3.5n-o(n)\), the bound from its previous ECCC version. The updated abstract pushes the number all the way to \(4n\). Previous bounds from the line of work it builds on, by Find, Golovnev, Hirsch and Kulikov (2023) and Li and Yang (2022), sat only a little above \(3n\). Against that, a jump to \(4n\) is a big leap in a field that usually advances in fractions of \(n\).

The weapon is the old workhorse, gate elimination: substitute values, watch gates vanish, repeat. The new idea is to make the substitutions on an underlying space of independent parameters that specify the input values, not on the input variables themselves. According to the abstract, this extra freedom lets the authors get past certain bottleneck situations for gate elimination without making explicit substitutions.

There is also a bonus. The authors say the resulting proof is much simpler than those of Find–Golovnev–Hirsch–Kulikov and Li–Yang. A record bound that comes with a shorter proof suggests a technique with room left in it. Sources close to the gates report that nobody has yet asked how far the parameter trick can be pushed, but everyone is thinking it.

It is still linear, and still a long way from the superpolynomial bounds that \(\mathrm{P}\neq\mathrm{NP}\) would need. But for explicit functions against unrestricted circuits, linear is where the frontier is, and this week the frontier moved. Our perspective piece inside traces the half-century road to \(4n\).

COMMUNICATION COMPLEXITY

Connectivity Cracked After Four Decades

Zhang proves an optimal \(\Omega(N\log N)\) nondeterministic communication lower bound for connectivity of \(N\)-vertex graphs, up from Raz–Spieker's \(\Omega(N\log\log N)\). The paper also gives randomized bounds that are optimal for polynomially small error, and bounds that are optimal for every constant number of rounds.

The question has been open since Babai, Frankl and Simon (FOCS'86). Sending a spanning forest costs \(O(N\log N)\) bits, and Hajnal, Maass and Turán showed that this is optimal deterministically. Zhang [ECCC:TR26-269, arXiv:2610.32346] now proves it is optimal nondeterministically too. The bound comes from showing that \(1\)-monochromatic rectangles must be small.

The same ideas, applied to weighted, almost-monochromatic rectangles, give an \(\Omega(N\cdot\log(\min(1/\varepsilon,N)))\) bound for randomized protocols with error \(\varepsilon\), which is optimal for any polynomially small \(\varepsilon\). For constant error, \(r\)-round protocols need \(\Omega(N/r\cdot\log N)\) bits. Optimal bounds were previously known only for one and two rounds. The proof uses a new communication problem that composes inner product with pointer chasing.

The abstract says the results extend to other fundamental graph properties and show that known query and streaming algorithms are optimal. The general randomized case with constant error and unbounded rounds remains open.

AI-ASSISTED PROOF ALERT

Machine Cracks 1979 Formula Puzzle?

Jaakkola presents a proof, which he credits to the AI model GPT-Sol-6.1, that Minimum Equivalent Expression is \(\Sigma_2^p\)-complete under polynomial-time many-one reductions. This strengthens Buchfuhrer and Umans' 2008 result for Turing reductions. The claim awaits independent checking.

Given a propositional formula \(\varphi\) and a number \(k\), is \(\varphi\) equivalent to a formula with at most \(k\) variable occurrences? This problem, MEE, is clearly in \(\Sigma_2^p\). Whether it is complete under many-one reductions has been open since at least 1979. In 2008 Buchfuhrer and Umans proved completeness under Turing reductions.

Jaakkola [arXiv:2610.34516] now presents a short note closing the many-one gap. The note states plainly that the proof was discovered by GPT-Sol-6.1. This is a classic Boolean function complexity question, and if the argument holds up it is a clean resolution.

Caution, though: a decades-old open problem, a short note and a machine-found argument together call for careful refereeing. Until that happens, the paper treats this as a claim, and so do we.

CODING THEORY

42: The Answer to Gilbert–Varshamov

Cohen and Manket construct codes that beat the asymptotic Gilbert–Varshamov bound, the benchmark trade-off between rate and distance, over an alphabet of size 42.

Does the probabilistic method give the best possible codes? The Gilbert–Varshamov bound (1952, 1957) is the benchmark. In 1982, Tsfasman, Vladut and Zink combined Goppa's algebraic-geometric codes with deep algebraic geometry and number theory to beat it over sufficiently large fields.

Cohen and Manket [ECCC:TR26-272] push this to alphabet size 42. In the abstract's framing, this is a test case for how far algebraic structure can surpass random constructions. The abstract we received is truncated, so the details of the construction will have to wait for a closer read.

Our puzzle desk notes, purely as a joke, that 42 is already famous as the answer to life, the universe and everything. It now also answers a question about codes.

CIRCUIT LOWER BOUNDS: DOUBLE BILL

LOWER BOUND BONANZA! Gate Elimination Hits 4n as Constant-Depth Circuits Get Sharper Teeth

Mishra and Santhanam prove a \(4n-O(\log n)\) size lower bound for general \(B_2\) circuits computing an explicit function in \(\mathrm{P}\), up from \(3.5n-o(n)\) in their own earlier version. Li and Zhong show that every width-\(b\) CNF splits into at most \(2^{\varepsilon m}\) CNFs, each pinned down by a few satisfying assignments. They use this to prove \(2^{\Omega_d((n\log n)^{1/(d-1)})}\) lower bounds against depth-\(d\) AND/OR circuits, \(d\ge3\), for explicit polynomial-time functions, plus a correlation bound for inverse trace that improves on the classical parity bound.

Explicit circuit lower bounds are the trench warfare of complexity theory. Progress is measured in single constants, and every inch is fought for. This week the front line moved twice: once on general circuits and once at constant depth.

Round one: the 4n punch

The setting is the hardest one we have. These are general Boolean circuits over the full binary basis \(B_2\), where every fan-in-two gate is allowed. For such circuits, nobody can even prove that some explicit function needs a large linear number of gates.

Mishra and Santhanam [ECCC:TR26-260] now give a lower bound of \(4n-O(\log n)\) for an explicit Boolean function \(F\) in \(\mathrm{P}\).

Instead of substituting values for input variables, the authors substitute on an underlying space of independent parameters that specify the values of the inputs.

The paper's title still advertises \(3.5n-o(n)\). That figure was the bound in the previous ECCC version, and the authors explicitly improve on it in the current one. Sources close to the eprint server confirm that, for once, the headline undersells the paper.

The weapon is the oldest one in the armoury: gate elimination. You fix some inputs, simplify the circuit, and count how many gates vanish. Repeat until nothing is left.

The method builds heavily on earlier work by Find, Golovnev, Hirsch and Kulikov (2023) and by Li and Yang (2022). Its known weak spot is the bottleneck configuration, where no substitution kills enough gates.

The abstract names the conceptual twist. Instead of substituting values for input variables, the authors substitute on an underlying space of independent parameters that specify the values of the inputs. Working one level of abstraction up gives flexibility. Certain bottleneck situations can be handled without performing explicit substitutions at all.

The second dividend is simplicity. According to the authors, the resulting proof is much simpler than those of Find–Golovnev–Hirsch–Kulikov and Li–Yang. In a field where the previous records came with long case analyses, simpler machinery that also goes further is the real story.

Round two: CNFs, chopped and listed

At the other end of the ring, Li and Zhong [ECCC:TR26-261] (also posted as [arXiv:2610.27127]) attack constant-depth circuits. These are AND/OR circuits of unbounded fan-in with input negations, the classical \(\mathrm{AC}^0\) setting.

Their engine is a structural theorem about bounded-width CNFs, and CSP readers should sit up. Fix any \(\varepsilon>0\). Every width-\(b\) CNF on \(m\) variables is an exact disjoint union of at most \(2^{\varepsilon m}\) width-\(b\) CNFs. Each piece is completely determined by just \(2^{O_\varepsilon(b)}\log(m+1)\) of its satisfying assignments.

In other words, a bounded-width CNF can be cut into subexponentially many pieces, and each piece has a description of only logarithmic length: a short “point list”. The number of pieces, \(2^{\varepsilon m}\), has a familiar sparsification-lemma flavour. The abstract does not draw that connection; this is our observation, and we flag it as such.

The lower bounds

Fix \(d\ge3\). Li and Zhong prove a worst-case size lower bound of \(2^{\Omega_d((n\log n)^{1/(d-1)})}\) against depth-at-most-\(d\) circuits for three polynomial-time families:

  • inverse trace over binary fields;
  • the Goldreich–Tal trilinear function;
  • OpenAI's polynomial evaluator, with its original parameters.

All parameter bits count as inputs, so there is no smuggling of hardness through hard-wired advice. The proof combines the CNF decomposition with restriction arguments. The bound carries an extra \(\log n\) inside the exponent compared with the familiar \(n^{1/(d-1)}\) shape.

For inverse trace they also prove a correlation bound. Every depth-\(d\) circuit of size \(S\), with \(n\le S\le 2^{c_d n^{1/(d-1)}}\), has uniform sign correlation at most \(2^{-\eta_d n\log\log S/(\log S)^{d-1}}\). The authors state that this improves on the classical parity correlation bound. The correlation proof additionally uses common partial decision trees.

The twist: inverse trace is not parity

Here is the plot development a tabloid lives for. The authors also build upper bounds for inverse trace, implementing finite-field evaluation and interpolation with circuits that enumerate intermediate states:

  • size \(2^{O(n^{2/3})}\) at depth eight;
  • size \(2^{O(\sqrt n\log n)}\) at depth nine;
  • size \(2^{O_h(n^{1/h}\log n)}\) at depth \(4h+1\), for every fixed \(h\ge2\).

So inverse trace gets markedly easier as depth grows. At depth \(4h+1\) the lower bound is roughly \(2^{(n\log n)^{1/(4h)}}\), while the upper bound is roughly \(2^{n^{1/h}}\). Between the two lies a polynomial gap in the exponent. Where the true answer sits is wide open.

What it all means

The two papers share a philosophy: change the space you reason in. Mishra and Santhanam substitute on parameters rather than variables. Li and Zhong replace an arbitrary CNF with a short list of points.

Neither paper threatens the big walls, and the abstracts claim nothing of the sort. Linear-size bounds remain far from the superpolynomial bounds needed for \(\mathrm{P}\ne\mathrm{NP}\). Depth-\(d\) bounds of this kind say nothing about \(\mathrm{ACC}\) or beyond.

The open questions are concrete:

  • How far can parameter-space gate elimination be pushed before it meets its own bottleneck?
  • Does the point-list decomposition have life beyond restrictions, for instance in proof complexity or in fine-grained algorithms for CNF-SAT?
  • What is the true constant-depth complexity of inverse trace?

For now, the trench has moved. Bring sandbags.

PARALLEL REPETITION SPECIAL

MANY PLAYERS, ONE INEQUALITY! Braverman and Minzer Tame Parallel Repetition for Every k-Player XOR Game

Braverman and Minzer prove that for every \(k\)-player XOR game over \(\mathbb{F}_p\) with value strictly less than 1, the \(n\)-fold parallel repetition has value at most \(2^{-cn}\), where \(c>0\) depends only on the game. This extends earlier exponential-decay results of Bhangale, Braverman, Khot, Liu and Minzer, which covered only some 3-XOR games. The proof maps the game to a \(2k\)-player game whose commuting value is below 1, then tensorizes a Cauchy–Schwarz argument.

Play a game once and you might get lucky. Play it \(n\) times in parallel and, if the universe is fair, your luck should run out exponentially fast.

For two-player games, that intuition has been a theorem for a long time. Once a third player joins, the picture becomes murkier. This week Braverman and Minzer [ECCC:TR26-285] [arXiv:2610.30455] clear the fog for an entire family of multiplayer games: every \(k\)-player XOR game over \(\mathbb{F}_p\).

The game on the table

The setup will feel familiar to anyone who has spent time with linear equations as constraints. A verifier samples a tuple of challenges \((x_1,\ldots,x_k)\) from a distribution \(\mu\) over \(\Sigma_1\times\cdots\times\Sigma_k\). Player \(i\) sees only \(x_i\) and replies with \(f_i(x_i)\in\mathbb{F}_p\).

Proving a relaxation small is harder than proving the real value small, but the payoff is a bound built from a single inequality, and such a bound can be copied coordinate by coordinate.

The verifier accepts exactly when

\(f_1(x_1)+\cdots+f_k(x_k)=t(x_1,\ldots,x_k)\)

for a fixed target map \(t\). In CSP language, each round is a random \(k\)-ary linear equation over \(\mathbb{F}_p\). The players' strategies are an assignment, split across \(k\) separate “variable blocks”. The value of the game is the best acceptance probability the players can achieve.

In the repeated game \(\Psi^{\otimes n}\), the verifier runs \(n\) independent copies at once. Each player receives all \(n\) of their challenges together and must answer all of them, and the players win only if every copy accepts. The danger, and the reason parallel repetition is subtle, is that a player can correlate answers across copies, so the value need not simply multiply.

The result

The theorem is clean. If \(\Psi\) has value strictly less than 1, then

\(\mathrm{val}(\Psi^{\otimes n})\le 2^{-cn}\)

for a constant \(c=c(\Psi)>0\).

That is exponential decay, with no restriction on \(k\), the prime \(p\), the alphabets, or the target map beyond the value being below 1.

According to the abstract, this extends a previous result of Bhangale, Braverman, Khot, Liu and Minzer, which established exponential decay for some 3-XOR games. The new work upgrades “some” to “all”, and “3” to “any \(k\)”.

The trick: go quantum, then double the players

The most eye-catching part is the route. Braverman and Minzer pass through the commuting value, a relaxation of the game value studied in the quantum setting.

The argument has three moves.

Step one: a transformation. Starting from a \(k\)-player game \(\Psi\) with value below 1, they build a \(2k\)-player game \(\Psi'\) whose commuting value is below 1. The key property is that the original repeated game is dominated by the new one:

\(\mathrm{val}(\Psi^{\otimes n})\le \mathrm{val}((\Psi')^{\otimes n})\).

Step two: a one-line certificate. They show that the bound for \(\Psi'\) has a direct proof using “nothing more than the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.”

Step three: tensorize. Because the certificate is just Cauchy–Schwarz, it behaves well under tensor products. That lets them lift it to the \(n\)-fold repetition, which yields the exponential decay.

The philosophy is worth savouring. Proving that a relaxation is small is generally harder than proving that the actual value is small. The payoff is structure: a bound that comes from a simple inequality can be copied coordinate by coordinate. Doubling the number of players is the price of reaching a game where such a simple certificate exists.

The abstract does not spell out how the doubling works. Sources close to the blackboard suggest it involves each player acquiring an evil twin, but readers should treat that as tabloid speculation and consult the paper.

Why a CSP person should care

Parallel repetition is the classic amplifier in the PCP toolkit. You start with a small gap between “satisfiable” and “not quite satisfiable”. You repeat. The gap grows into the strong soundness that hardness reductions feed on.

Multiplayer games correspond to PCPs and constraint systems where a check reads more than two oracle positions. XOR games are, quite literally, linear-equation constraints over a finite field.

The abstract makes no specific hardness-of-approximation claims, so we will not invent any. Still, a general exponential-decay theorem for every \(k\)-player XOR game is the kind of robust, assumption-free amplification that can make multiplayer reductions behave like their two-player cousins. That matters for anyone who thinks about linear-equation CSPs, promise versions of them, or the multiplayer machinery behind such reductions.

What stays open

Several questions remain on the table.

  • How big is \(c\)? The constant \(c(\Psi)\) depends on the game. The abstract gives no explicit dependence on \(k\), \(p\), the alphabet sizes, or how far the value sits below 1. Quantitative bounds are what downstream reductions will crave.
  • Beyond XOR. The argument is tailored to predicates of the form \(\sum_i f_i(x_i)=t\). Whether the commuting-value route reaches other multiplayer predicates is not addressed in the abstract.
  • The doubling. It is natural to ask whether moving from \(k\) to \(2k\) players is essential, or just a convenient bridge to a world where Cauchy–Schwarz suffices.

For now, the headline stands. One of the most stubborn corners of parallel repetition, multiplayer games, has yielded an unconditional exponential-decay theorem for a whole natural class. And the closing blow was delivered by an inequality every undergraduate knows.

PROOF COMPLEXITY

WHO PROVES THE PROVERS? Logicians Put Algebraic Lower Bounds on the Witness Stand, and Find They Need NC² Brains to Testify

Garlik, Gryaznov, Lu, Santhanam and Tzameret open the study of the meta-mathematics of algebraic circuit lower bounds. Contemporary rank-based lower bounds are hard for Polynomial Calculus Resolution, yet efficiently provable in \(\mathsf{NC}^2\)-level reasoning: the theory \(\mathsf{VNC}^2\) and quasipolynomial-size Frege. Constant-depth algebraic reasoning is likely too weak even for constant-depth lower bounds. Under an algebraic analogue of the Krajíček–Razborov conjecture, this suggests that general superpolynomial algebraic lower bounds need stronger reasoning than today's rank methods.

Complexity theorists spend their lives proving that things are hard. A new paper by Garlik, Gryaznov, Lu, Santhanam and Tzameret [arXiv:2610.33938] turns the spotlight around. It asks how hard it is to prove that things are hard.

Their target is algebraic circuit lower bounds, the arithmetic cousins of the Boolean bounds this paper covers every week. The question is old in spirit and new in this setting. What logical strength do our lower-bound arguments actually use? And what strength would a proof of the big open lower bounds require?

The authors say they initiate this study. They pursue two goals. The first is insight into which methods are sufficient, and which are necessary, for algebraic lower bounds. The second is to contribute to bounded arithmetic as a logical foundation for complexity lower bounds. A preliminary version appeared at LICS 2026.

The tools that have powered recent progress, when viewed as formal proofs, live in a theory that is plausibly too weak for the main prize.

The question behind the question

Proof complexity has long treated circuit lower bounds as objects of study in their own right. The idea is to write the statement “this polynomial has no small circuit” as a family of formal statements. Then you ask which proof systems, or which weak theories of arithmetic, can prove them efficiently.

If a weak theory proves a lower bound, the argument behind it is “low-tech” in a precise sense. If a weak theory provably cannot, then any proof must use stronger ideas.

In the Boolean world this line of thought is tied to a family of conjectures associated with Krajíček and Razborov. Roughly, they say that certain weak systems should not be able to prove strong circuit lower bounds. The new paper works under an algebraic analogue of that conjecture. The exact formulation is in the paper, not the abstract.

Three verdicts

The abstract ends with “Specifically, we show the following” before the formal theorems. So what follows are the headline claims, not the fine print.

Verdict one: too hard for PCR. Algebraic circuit lower bounds are hard for somewhat weak proof systems such as Polynomial Calculus Resolution (\(\mathsf{PCR}\)). PCR reasons with polynomial equations and has its own large literature of resolution-style lower bounds. Algebraic reasoning at that level cannot efficiently certify algebraic hardness.

Verdict two: easy for \(\mathsf{NC}^2\) reasoning. Contemporary lower bounds are efficiently provable in proof systems and bounded arithmetic theories corresponding to \(\mathsf{NC}^2\). The paper names the theory \(\mathsf{VNC}^2\) and the matching propositional system, Frege proofs of quasipolynomial size. So today's arguments sit comfortably in a theory whose reasoning power matches \(\mathsf{NC}^2\), a modest level of the complexity world.

Verdict three: constant depth is likely not enough. Go below \(\mathsf{VNC}^2\) into algebraic constant-depth reasoning, and efficient proofs are likely out of reach. This holds even for lower bounds against constant-depth algebraic circuits. The abstract hedges here, and so do we. As far as the abstract reveals, the evidence is suggestive rather than unconditional.

Why NC² is the natural home

The abstract describes the arguments under study as rank-based proofs. Many celebrated algebraic lower bounds follow one template. Attach a matrix to a polynomial, show that small circuits can only produce low-rank matrices, then exhibit a polynomial whose matrix has high rank.

The abstract does not explain why \(\mathsf{VNC}^2\) suffices. A natural reading is that the logic only needs to reason about rank, and rank-style linear algebra is the kind of computation an \(\mathsf{NC}^2\)-strength theory can handle. Treat that as our gloss, not a statement from the paper. Sources close to the newsroom's linear-algebra desk insist that everything is secretly Gaussian elimination. This is a joke.

The conditional moral

The punchline is conditional. Assume the algebraic Krajíček–Razborov-type conjecture holds. Then superpolynomial lower bounds for general algebraic circuits will require stronger reasoning than the rank-based proofs studied here.

That is a barrier result in the meta-mathematical style. It does not say general lower bounds are impossible. It says the tools that have powered recent progress, when viewed as formal proofs, live in a theory that is plausibly too weak for the main prize.

For readers who track both proof complexity and circuit lower bounds, the appeal is that two programmes merge. The question what can PCR or Frege prove? becomes a tool for understanding the limits of lower-bound techniques. It is no longer only a question about refuting unsatisfiable formulas.

What's still open

Plenty.

  • The conjecture itself. The algebraic Krajíček–Razborov analogue carries the conditional conclusion, and it is a conjecture.
  • Hedged, not proved. The verdict on constant-depth algebraic reasoning is stated as likely. Turning that into an unconditional unprovability result would sharpen the picture considerably.
  • Beyond rank. The paper analyses rank-based proofs. Whether other, non-rank lower-bound strategies escape \(\mathsf{VNC}^2\) is exactly the kind of question this framework now lets one pose precisely.
  • The Boolean bridge. The authors frame the work as a contribution to bounded arithmetic as a foundation for complexity lower bounds generally. How far these algebraic results transfer back to the Boolean side is an open invitation.

One line of the abstract sums up the mood: we now have the first map of which logics our best algebraic lower bounds actually need. The territory beyond rank-based methods is still blank.

FROM 2n TO 4n: FIFTY YEARS OF HACKING AWAY AT CIRCUIT SIZE, ONE GATE AT A TIME

A fresh, not-yet-refereed ECCC revision by Mishra and Santhanam claims a \(4n-O(\log n)\) lower bound on the size of \(B_2\) circuits for an explicit function in \(\mathrm{P}\). If it holds, it caps a gate-elimination lineage that ran from Schnorr's \(2n\) through Blum's long-lived \(3n\) to Li and Yang's \(3.1n\).

Here is the most embarrassing number in complexity theory, which also makes for a gripping story. Shannon showed in 1949 that almost all Boolean functions need circuits of size about \(2^n/n\) [S2][S3]. Yet nobody can prove even a superlinear lower bound for an explicit function, not even for functions in \(\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{NP}}\) [S2]. Over the full binary basis \(B_2\), where any two-input function is a gate and size counts gates, the whole fight has been over the leading constant in \(cn\).

This week Mishra and Santhanam [ECCC:TR26-260] moved that constant. Their first ECCC version claimed \(3.5n-o(n)\). A revision posted days later claims \(4n-O(\log n)\) for an explicit \(F \in \mathrm{P}\) [S1]. It is a preprint, not peer reviewed, so treat it as a claim for now. Here is the road that led to it.

Two gates per variable (1965–1974)

The first \(2n\)-type bound is credited to Kloss and Malyshev in 1965. They proved \(2n-O(1)\) for \(\bigoplus_{i<j} x_i x_j\) [S2]. We have this only through later citations.

The template everyone still uses came from Schnorr in 1974 [S4]. He proved \(2n-O(1)\) for every function that has at least three distinct subfunctions whenever any two variables are fixed to constants [S2]. The crucial move was to make each substitution land the function back inside the same class [S2]. Then you can run induction: kill some gates, recurse, repeat. That loop is gate elimination.

The 2.5n era (1977)

Two papers from 1977 pushed to \(2.5n\).

Stockmeyer proved \(2.5n-O(1)\) for certain symmetric functions, including \(\mathrm{MOD}_m\) for constant \(m \ge 3\) [S2][S5][S3]. His new step was a paired substitution, \(x_i \leftarrow f\) together with \(x_j \leftarrow f \oplus 1\). This removes five gates over two substitutions. Because it forces \(x_i + x_j = 1\), a \(\mathrm{MOD}_3\) function stays a \(\mathrm{MOD}_3\) function [S3].

Paul got \(2.5n-o(n)\) for a function built from storage-access functions [S2]. He introduced “linear reconstruction”, which rearranges blocks of XOR gates. Stockmeyer, Blum and much later Find et al. all reused it [S2].

Blum's wall (1984)

In 1984 Blum proved \(3n-o(n)\) for an artificial function on \(n + 3\log n + 3\) variables [S3]. The function is universal: suitable settings of the control bits make the output equal \(x_i \wedge x_j\) or \(x_i \oplus x_j\) for any pair of inputs. Blum substitutes arbitrary functions for the data variables, and he counts high-fan-out gates whenever an elimination step falls short [S3].

That bound stood for more than thirty years [S2][S6][S7]. Golovnev, Hirsch, Knop and Kulikov later explained why the classical classes were stuck: each one contains functions with small circuits [S3].

  • \(\mathrm{THR}_2\) sits in Schnorr's class and has size \(2n+o(n)\).
  • \(\mathrm{MOD}_4\) sits in Stockmeyer's class at \(2.5n+\Theta(1)\).
  • Blum's own function has circuits of size \(6n+o(n)\) [S3].

To go further, you needed a class with no small members that also survives many substitutions.

Enter the affine dispersers (2011–2022)

Demenkov and Kulikov (2011) supplied that class: affine dispersers of sublinear dimension [S8]. Such a function is non-constant on every affine subspace of dimension \(d = o(n)\). It therefore survives \(n - o(n)\) affine substitutions [S8][S3].

They tracked the measure \(\mu = gates + inputs\) and showed that some affine substitution always drops \(\mu\) by at least 4. That gives \(gates \ge 3n - o(n)\) [S3]. This matched Blum with a much simpler proof, though with a worse \(o(n)\) term [S2].

Find, Golovnev, Hirsch and Kulikov broke through \(3n\). Their result appeared on ECCC in 2015 [S9], at FOCS 2016 [S10] and in Computational Complexity in 2023 [S11]. They proved \((3 + \tfrac{1}{86})n - o(n)\) for affine dispersers [S2][S11], using three ingredients [S2]:

  • they allowed cyclic circuits, so affine rewiring never increases the gate count;
  • they used a potential function that charges for “troubled gates”;
  • they made quadratic substitutions such as \(z = xy\), treated as delayed affine ones.

The journal version adds a simpler \(3.11n\) proof for quadratic dispersers, but nobody yet knows how to construct those explicitly [S11].

Li and Yang reached \(3.1n - o(n)\) in 2021–22 [S6][S12]. Their finer case analysis creates far fewer bottlenecks. It rests on three observations: adjacent bottlenecks are less troublesome, eliminated gates tend to be connected, and the worst cases have local structure that blocks new bottlenecks [S6].

A word of warning for readers who remember “5n”. Over \(U_2\), which is \(B_2\) without XOR and XNOR, Iwama and Morizumi reached \(5n - o(n)\) [S2]. Over \(B_2\) the record sat at \(3n\) until 2016 [S2][S5].

The ceiling looms

In 2016–18 Golovnev, Hirsch, Knop and Kulikov built the method's first barrier [S3][S7][S13]. They composed functions with “stable” gadgets. For the resulting functions, a constant number of substitutions of any type lowers any subadditive measure by only a constant. For measures of the form \(gates + \alpha \cdot inputs\), this gives an explicit ceiling of \(cn\) [S3].

Later, Golovnev and Kulikov summarised the line as showing that gate elimination is “too weak to prove a 5n lower bound” [S14]. That statement applies to one specific formalization.

A \(4n\) result sits uncomfortably close to that line. The authors of the barrier suggest several escapes [S3]:

  • induction on arbitrary varieties such as \(xy = zt\);
  • local measures;
  • showing that natural classes resist the gadget trick.

They also offered a non-elimination route: every size-\(s\) circuit is an OR of \(2^{s/3.9}\) 16-CNFs [S14]. Meanwhile, Li and Yang note that nobody knows whether any explicit function needs more than \(10n\) gates [S6].

This week: substituting on parameters, not variables

Mishra and Santhanam say they build heavily on Find et al. and on Li and Yang [S1]. Their stated twist is to perform substitutions on an underlying space of independent parameters that specify input values, rather than on the inputs themselves. This lets them handle bottleneck situations without making explicit substitutions, and they claim a much simpler proof as a bonus [S1]. The ECCC keywords list affine dispersers [S1], but the abstract alone does not pin down the function. Watch this space for the referees.

Why formulas ran away

For contrast, formula lower bounds have soared, all the way to Tal's \(\Omega(n^3/(\log^2 n \cdot \log^3\log n))\) for Andreev's function [S15]. They rest on shrinkage under random restrictions, which works for tree-shaped formulas [S15]. Find et al. stress that these bounds “do not translate” to general circuits [S2]. Our own gloss: in a circuit, fan-out lets a single input reach many gates at once, so a random restriction cannot simply be averaged over a tree. That is why the circuit world still grinds forward one gate per variable at a time.

The technique: Gate elimination

Idea. Take an optimal circuit for a function in a “hard class” and substitute something for one input: a constant, an affine form, or in newer proofs a quadratic or arbitrary function. Every gate fed by that input now computes a constant or a function of a single wire, so it can be deleted.

If each step deletes at least \(c\) gates and the restricted function stays in the class for \(k\) steps, induction gives roughly \(ck\) gates [S3][S2]. The art lies in choosing the class and the substitution so the class is closed under the operation and the circuit always loses enough.

Toy run: Schnorr's \(2n-4\) for \(\mathrm{MOD}_3\) (as presented in [S3]). Let \(\mathrm{MOD}^n_{3,r}(x) = 1\) iff \(\sum x_i \equiv r \pmod 3\).

1. Let \(G\) be the topologically first gate, with distinct input variables \(x_i\) and \(x_j\). 2. Suppose both \(x_i\) and \(x_j\) had fan-out 1. Then the rest of the circuit sees them only through \(G\). The four settings of \((x_i, x_j)\) would yield at most 2 subfunctions. But \(\mathrm{MOD}_3\) yields 3: \(\mathrm{MOD}^{n-2}_{3,0}\), \(\mathrm{MOD}^{n-2}_{3,1}\) and \(\mathrm{MOD}^{n-2}_{3,2}\). Contradiction. 3. So, say, \(x_i\) has fan-out \(\ge 2\). Set \(x_i \leftarrow 0\). Both successor gates of \(x_i\) become trivial and vanish, and the circuit now computes \(\mathrm{MOD}^{n-1}_{3,r}\), which is still in the class. 4. Hence \(s(n) \ge s(n-1) + 2 \ge 2(n-1) - 4 + 2 = 2n - 4\).

For \(n = 3\) the bound gives 2, and indeed \(\mathrm{MOD}^3_{3,0} = \neg((x_1 \oplus x_2) \vee (x_2 \oplus x_3))\) uses 3 gates.

Why it stalls. Constant substitutions alone cannot pass \(2n\) for this class, because \(\mathrm{THR}_2\) lies in it and has \(2n + o(n)\) circuits [S3]. Every later record needed new ingredients: amortised steps, affine and quadratic substitutions, and weighted measures like \(gates + \alpha \cdot inputs\) [S3]. This week's version moves the substitutions onto a space of independent parameters [S1].
