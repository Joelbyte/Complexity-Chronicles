PROOF COMPLEXITY

First hard instances for AC0[2]-Frege at every depth

For every fixed \(d \ge 2\), explicit depth-\(d\) formulas need superpolynomial-size semantic depth-\(d\) \(AC^0[2]\)-Frege refutations. Semantic depth-3 and depth-4 polynomial calculus over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) also receive exponential and superpolynomial lower bounds, which settles the Res(log) question for the weak pigeonhole principle.

Alekseev, Gryaznov, Itsykson, Pitassi, Robere and Tzameret prove size lower bounds against a ladder of algebraic-Boolean proof systems [ECCC:TR26-316]. Every bound is for the semantic version of the system, over \(\mathbb{F}_2\).

The first rung is \(\Sigma\Pi\Sigma\)-PC, polynomial calculus whose lines are depth-3 circuits with a sum gate on top. Here the bounds are exponential: for the bit pigeonhole principle, and \(2^{\Omega(n)}\) for random 3-CNFs on \(n\) variables and for the bit and functional pigeonhole principles with \(n+1\) pigeons and \(n\) holes. The second rung is \(\Sigma\Pi\Sigma\Pi\)-PC with unrestricted bottom fan-in. Here the bounds are superpolynomial, for the weak unary pigeonhole principle and for lifted random CNFs of logarithmic width. With \(2n\) pigeons and \(n\) holes the bound is \(\exp(\Omega((\log n)^2/\log\log n))\). This answers the open question of a superpolynomial Res(log) lower bound for that principle. If the bottom fan-in is held to logarithmic, the bounds for random CNFs of logarithmic width become exponential.

The top rung is \(AC^0[2]\)-Frege. For each fixed \(d \ge 2\) the authors build unsatisfiable formulas of depth \(d\). These need superpolynomial-size semantic depth-\(d\) refutations, yet have polynomial-size refutations at depth \(d+1\). They are the first unconditional hard instances against \(AC^0[2]\)-Frege of arbitrary depth. The instances are deep formulas, not CNFs. For CNFs, the strongest system the paper reaches is depth-four polynomial calculus.

The method starts from Braun's recent AI-generated proof of an exponential lower bound for resolution over parities. The authors distil the annihilating-polynomial argument implicit in that proof into a general framework built on pseudo-reduction operators. Within it, rank lower bounds for these operators give proof-size lower bounds. The Razborov–Smolensky polynomial approximation method extends the framework to bounded bottom degree, and lifting with random restrictions removes the bound on bottom fan-in. The \(AC^0[2]\)-Frege result adds a lifting construction based on constant-depth formulas for the coin problem.

The weak pigeonhole bound is superpolynomial, which is what the question asked for. It is not exponential.

[ECCC:TR26-316] Hard Instances for Strong Semantic Proof Systems: CNFs for Depth-Four PC and Deep Instances for AC0[2]-Frege (Yaroslav Alekseev, Svyatoslav Gryaznov, Dmitry Itsykson, Toniann Pitassi et al.)

FINE-GRAINED

Claimed 3-SAT savings carry over to NTIME

If the subexponential 3-SAT algorithms reported by OpenAI and Anthropic are correct, then \(\mathrm{NTIME}[t]\subseteq\mathrm{DTIME}[2^{O(t\log\log t/\sqrt{\log t})}]\), and a stronger consequence of their proof gives \(\mathrm{NTIME}[t]\subseteq\mathrm{DTIME}[2^{O(t/\sqrt{\log t})}]\) for multitape machines.

Strassle and Subercaseaux show that the 3-SAT algorithms recently reported by OpenAI and Anthropic imply \(\mathrm{NTIME}[t]\subseteq\mathrm{DTIME}[2^{O(t\log\log t/\sqrt{\log t})}]\) [arXiv:2611.11832]. A stronger consequence of the same proof removes the \(\log\log t\) factor. More generally, any subexponential algorithm for circuit-CSPs becomes a deterministic simulation of nondeterministic time, with the same savings.

The argument rests on new generalisations of the Cook–Levin theorem, which the authors offer as of independent interest. These carry a saving on circuit-CSPs over to multitape nondeterministic Turing machines without losing it.

The result is conditional in the plain sense: it holds if the reported algorithms are correct. The saving in the exponent is a factor of order \(\sqrt{\log t}\).

[arXiv:2611.11832] Simulating Nondeterministic Turing Machines in Subexponential Deterministic Time (Carmen Strassle, Bernardo Subercaseaux)

VALUED CSP

One number fixes the treewidth cost of valued CSPs

For finite-valued VCSPs whose language contains all crisp unary constraints, instances of treewidth \(\mathsf{tw}\) can be solved in time \((c_\Gamma)^{\mathsf{tw}}\) times a polynomial. When \(c_\Gamma\ge2\), no \((c_\Gamma-\varepsilon)^{\mathsf{tw}}\) algorithm exists unless the Primal Treewidth SETH, and hence CNF-SETH, fails.

Greilhuber, Marx, Osipov, Piecyk, Rzążewski and Živný study valued CSPs over a fixed finite domain [arXiv:2611.16729]. The language contains every crisp unary constraint, called a hard list, and all its other cost functions are finite-valued. The parameter that matters is \(c_\Gamma\). This is the size of the largest set \(S\) on which the language can express a binary disequality, using the usual VCSP closure operations.

Given a tree decomposition of width \(\mathsf{tw}\) of the primal graph, the problem is solvable in time \((c_\Gamma)^{\mathsf{tw}}\cdot(|I|+|\mathcal{T}|)^{O(1)}\). For \(c_\Gamma\ge2\), the base cannot be lowered to \(c_\Gamma-\varepsilon\) for any \(\varepsilon>0\) unless the Primal Treewidth SETH fails. This generalises a result of Esmer et al. on the optimisation version of list homomorphism for graphs.

The lower bound needs only the Primal Treewidth SETH, which is weaker than CNF-SETH. That choice matters in a season of reported ETH refutations. Because SETH implies ETH, a refutation of ETH would also refute CNF-SETH. Whether the weaker hypothesis survives is a separate question.

[arXiv:2611.16729] Tight Algorithms for Conservative Finite-Valued CSPs Parameterized by Treewidth (Jakob Greilhuber, Dániel Marx, George Osipov, Marta Piecyk et al.)

PARAMETERISED SAT

Forest backdoors are FPT; treewidth two is not

A strong backdoor of size at most \(k\) to the class of incidence forests can be found, or shown not to exist, in time \(2^{2^{O(k)}}|\varphi|\) on any non-tautological CNF. For treewidth \(t\ge2\) the problem is W[1]-hard even on 3-CNFs, and any better dependence on \(k\) would give a \(2^{o(N/\log N)}\)-time 3-SAT algorithm.

In 2013 Gaspers and Szeider gave an FPT approximation for strong backdoors to bounded incidence treewidth. When a backdoor of size \(k\) exists, their algorithm finds one of size at most \(2^k\), in time \(f(k,t)|\varphi|^3\). Whether exact detection is FPT in \(k+t\) was left open, even for forests and even for 3-CNF inputs. Bilyk, Munduzbaev, Sharma and Siam settle it [arXiv:2611.12321].

For forests the answer is yes. On arbitrary non-tautological CNFs, their algorithm runs in time \(2^{2^{O(k)}}|\varphi|\), which is linear in the input. For treewidth \(t\ge2\), the problem is W[1]-hard, even on non-tautological 3-CNFs.

The double-exponential dependence on \(k\) is also shown to be needed. A better one would give a \(2^{o(N/\log N)}\)-time algorithm for 3-SAT on \(N\) variables. The authors note that this would beat even the recently claimed subexponential 3-SAT algorithms.

[arXiv:2611.12321] Strong Backdoor to Forests is FPT: Tight Linear-Time FPT for Essentially General SAT (Olesia Bilyk, Aidar Munduzbaev, Roohani Sharma, Mamnoon Siam)

Fine-grained complexity and proof complexity

What subexponential 3-SAT buys

If the reported OpenAI and Anthropic algorithms are correct, then every multitape nondeterministic machine running in time \(t\) can be simulated deterministically in time \(2^{O(t/\sqrt{\log t})}\). Separately, under three stated assumptions from the Anthropic preprint, every unsatisfiable 3-CNF on \(n\) variables has an Ideal Proof System refutation of size \(2^{o(n)}\) over any field of characteristic other than 2. Known lower bounds rule out the same for resolution, Res(\(k\)), polynomial calculus and sum-of-squares. Neither result beats brute force at any size that could be run.

Two preprints, one from OpenAI and one from Anthropic, report deterministic algorithms that count the satisfying assignments of a 3-CNF on \(n\) variables in time \(2^{o(n)}\). If they are correct, ETH is false. Neither preprint has been refereed. This week two papers ask what follows. One looks at nondeterministic time. The other looks at proof complexity and knowledge compilation. Both are conditional, and both say so.

Why Cook–Levin was not enough

The first instinct is to push \(\mathrm{NTIME}[t]\) through Cook–Levin and run the new algorithm on the result. Strassle and Subercaseaux [arXiv:2611.11832] explain why this fails. The best known Cook–Levin reduction produces a 3-CNF with \(O(t \log t)\) variables. A \(2^{O(n/\sqrt{\log n})}\) algorithm on that many variables gives time \(2^{O(t\sqrt{\log t})}\), which is worse than the trivial \(2^{O(t)}\). Making Cook–Levin linear is a long-standing open problem.

Their answer is to avoid needing it. They prove \(\mathrm{NTIME}[t] \subseteq \mathrm{DTIME}[2^{O(t/\sqrt{\log t})}]\), given a version of the reported algorithms that solves circuit-CSPs: constraint problems in which each constraint is an arbitrary Boolean circuit on its variables. From the 3-SAT statement alone they get the weaker \(2^{O(t \log\log t/\sqrt{\log t})}\). More generally, any subexponential saving for circuit-CSPs of bounded density and suitable arity transfers to \(\mathrm{NTIME}\) with the same saving.

There are \(2^{O(t/b)}\) possible patterns, and the simulation tries them all.

This answers, conditionally, a conjecture attributed to Lipton that \(\mathrm{NTIME}[t] \subseteq \mathrm{DTIME}[2^{\varepsilon t}]\) for every \(\varepsilon > 0\). There is an oracle relative to which that conjecture fails, so the argument cannot relativize. The authors note that the statement also fails relative to a random oracle.

Cutting the run into epochs

The idea is to encode the computation in pieces. Split the run into epochs of length \(b\) and each tape into blocks of \(b\) cells. By a classical result of Hopcroft, Paul and Valiant, the machine may be assumed to move its heads between blocks only at epoch boundaries. Once the pattern of block moves is fixed, each epoch becomes one constraint circuit of size \(O(b \log b)\). That circuit links the block contents before and after the epoch with the \(b\) nondeterministic bits used during it. Each variable is shared by at most two constraints. There are \(2^{O(t/b)}\) possible patterns, and the simulation tries them all.

Each pattern gives a circuit-CSP with \(O(t)\) variables, arity \(O(b)\) and density at most 2. Converting each circuit to clauses gives a 3-CNF with \(O(t \log b)\) variables rather than \(O(t \log t)\). With \(b = t\) the classical reduction comes back. The authors compare the construction to the sparsification lemma. The cost is a family of \(2^{O(t/b)}\) formulas instead of one. That is affordable precisely when \(b\) is large compared with the savings of the solver.

Refutations from counting identities

Szeider [zenodo:24243988] starts from the identities the Anthropic preprint uses to prove its counting algorithm correct, not from the algorithm itself. He takes three statements from that proof as assumptions. Under them, he shows that the weighted model count of every 3-CNF on \(n\) variables has an arithmetic circuit of size \(2^{o(n)}\) over \(\mathbb{Z}[1/2]\). The circuit can be chosen smooth and decomposable.

The proof-complexity consequence uses a certificate from Grochow and Pitassi. Its coefficient polynomials, one per clause, are weighted model counts of prefixes of the formula. Small circuits for those counts give Hilbert-like IPS refutations of size \(2^{o(n)}\) for every unsatisfiable 3-CNF, over any field of characteristic other than 2. Characteristic 0 is only sketched, from a weaker algorithm in the OpenAI preprint. Extended Frege inherits the bound only under a further simulation hypothesis.

Lean 4 checks the circuit construction, the IPS size bound from the three assumptions, the DNNF lemma and the monotone lower bound for the clique polynomial. The corollaries on Extended Frege and on Tseitin formulas are proved on paper.

Where the bound cannot hold

The note lists the systems that cannot do this. Resolution, Res(\(k\)) for fixed \(k\), polynomial calculus and sum-of-squares all have \(2^{\Omega(n)}\) lower bounds on 3-CNFs with \(O(n)\) clauses. Cutting planes and bounded-depth Frege have no such known bound, and the note leaves them as open problems. This week Alekseev and coauthors [ECCC:TR26-316] prove new lower bounds for semantic polynomial calculus over depth-3 and depth-4 circuits and for \(AC^0[2]\)-Frege. Read together, the two papers narrow the gap from both sides.

The knowledge-compilation result separates monotone from non-monotone circuits. A monotone circuit for a weighted model count yields a complete DNNF with no more gates. Tseitin formulas of degree-3 expanders need \(2^{\Omega(n)}\) gates as DNNFs. The new circuits for them are smaller, so they must use negative constants. Cancellation also explains why the natural soundness invariant fails. Intermediate values need not be counts of anything, and a test found a network whose value is \(-1\).

What is not shown

Both algorithms are galactic. By the parameter choices in the preprints, OpenAI's algorithm is exhaustive search on every formula with fewer than \(2^{131071}\) variables and clauses. Anthropic's proved bound does not beat exhaustive search unless \(n > 10^{10000}\). The simulation and the refutations inherit this. Szeider states that he establishes no improvement over exhaustive search at physical sizes. A deterministic simulation of nondeterministic time is a large statement to rest on two unrefereed preprints. Its value at present lies in the reduction, not in the bound.

The open questions are concrete. Does Extended Frege refute every unsatisfiable 3-CNF in size \(2^{o(n)}\) without the extra simulation hypothesis? Which is the weakest standard proof system that does? Can the circuits avoid all constants except \(0\), \(1\) and \(-1\)? And can Cook–Levin be made linear, which would let a 3-SAT saving alone transfer without loss?

Algebraic approach

Maltsev recognition is in polynomial time

Delic and Syed give a polynomial-time algorithm that decides whether a finite relational structure, with relation arities bounded by a fixed constant, admits a Maltsev polymorphism. The algorithm needs no promise and no prior knowledge of the structure's polymorphisms.

A Maltsev polymorphism is a ternary operation \(m\) that preserves every relation of a structure and satisfies \(m(x,x,y) = m(y,x,x) = y\). Its presence has a well-known consequence: every CSP with that structure as template is solvable in polynomial time. So the question “does this template have a Maltsev polymorphism?” is a natural way to recognise one class of tractable constraint languages. In [arXiv:2611.14379] Delic and Syed show that this question can itself be answered in polynomial time.

What was known

Questions of this kind are called metaproblems. The input is a template, not an instance, and the task is to decide whether it has a polymorphism satisfying given identities. The paper's own survey of the area is short. Deciding whether a structure has a semilattice polymorphism, a conservative semilattice polymorphism or a commutative semigroup polymorphism is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. Majority and near-unanimity polymorphisms of structures and digraphs have been studied in several papers. On the algebraic side, there is work on deciding whether an idempotent algebra, given by its operations, has a Maltsev term. What was missing, the authors say, was a polynomial procedure that starts from a relational structure alone.

How the algorithm works

The starting point is standard. The ternary polymorphisms of \(\mathbf{A}\) are exactly the solutions of a CSP instance whose variables are the triples in \(A^3\) and whose domain is \(A\). Adding unary constraints that fix the value at every triple of the form \((a,a,b)\) or \((b,a,a)\) to \(b\) leaves exactly the Maltsev polymorphisms. The metaproblem thus becomes a single CSP instance of size polynomial in \(|A|\) for bounded arity. A reduction due to Barto and Kozik then turns it into an equivalent instance with only binary constraints, over a power of \(A\); a structure has a Maltsev polymorphism exactly when its powers do. The difficulty is that no known algorithm for Maltsev CSPs applies directly. Those algorithms use the polymorphisms they are given. Here none are known in advance.

A property of relations, checkable in logarithmic space, is made to stand in for algebraic information that the algorithm never sees.

The authors replace that knowledge with rectangularity. If a Maltsev operation exists, every binary constraint is rectangular: whenever \((a,c)\), \((a,d)\) and \((b,d)\) are in it, so is \((b,c)\). This can be checked directly, and a failure ends the search. Once rectangularity holds, each constraint splits into a disjoint union of blocks, and the associated equivalence relations behave like congruences without any algebra being written down. Between simple pieces, a constraint is either a full product or the graph of an isomorphism. The algorithm builds a “triple graph” whose vertices are a variable with a pair of distinct values, and whose edges record constraints of the isomorphism kind. Connected components group the variables that must move together. The algorithm then tests each variable–pair combination by restricting domains along these components and running a symmetric \((1,2)\)-Datalog consistency check, discarding what fails, and repeating until nothing changes. The number of rounds is polynomial in \(|A|\).

Why it matters

For anyone who uses polymorphisms to classify templates, the gap between “the template is tractable if it has a Maltsev polymorphism” and “we can tell whether it does” has been real. This result closes that gap for Maltsev operations, at least for templates of bounded arity. It also contrasts with the semilattice case, where the corresponding question is \(\mathrm{NP}\)-complete. The structure of the argument is of separate interest. A property of relations, checkable in logarithmic space, is made to stand in for algebraic information that the algorithm never sees.

This week's other CSP item points the same way. The tight treewidth algorithm for finite-valued hard-list VCSPs [arXiv:2611.16729] is governed by a single parameter that is also read off what the language can express, namely the largest set on which it can express a binary disequality.

What remains open

The authors are explicit about the bound. Maltsev CSPs themselves can be solved in \(\#\mathrm{L}\), by an algorithm that counts within known minimal subalgebras. Here the minimal subalgebras are unknown, and the authors place the metaproblem only in polynomial time, which they call the sharpest bound they can obtain. Whether it lies in a logspace-type class is open.

Several other points are worth noting. The analysis fixes a bound on the arity of the relations, so the case where arity is part of the input is not covered by the stated bound. The stated aim of producing an explicit Maltsev polymorphism, not just a yes or no, is described as an ideal rather than a theorem. And the correctness argument reasons about minimal subalgebras of an algebra the algorithm cannot see; that is the step most worth checking. Whether the rectangularity technique carries over to other conditions, such as near-unanimity, is not addressed.

Structural complexity

Karp–Lipton, pushed to the floor

Hirsch and Volkovich define an input-oblivious generator–tester–decoder class \(\mathrm{OM}\), prove that it lies inside each of the three deepest known Karp–Lipton collapse classes, and show that \(\mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{P/poly}\) implies \(\mathrm{PH} = \mathrm{OM}\). They also prove \(\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{prS_2^P}} \subseteq \mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{prSBP}}\). Fixed-polynomial circuit lower bounds for \(\mathrm{OM}\) remain open.

Karp and Lipton proved that if \(\mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{P/poly}\), the polynomial hierarchy collapses to its second level. The theorem matters less for itself than for what it feeds. Kannan showed that \(\mathrm{PH}\) contains, for every \(k\), a language with no circuits of size \(n^k\). Combine the two and a win–win follows. Either \(\mathrm{NP}\) lacks polynomial-size circuits, or \(\mathrm{PH}\) falls into the collapse class, which then inherits Kannan's lower bound. The smaller the collapse class, the stronger the lower bound that falls out of it.

A line of smaller classes

So the collapse has been pushed down. The learning algorithm of Bshouty, Cleve, Gavaldà, Kannan and Tamon, as used by Köbler and Watanabe, gave \(\mathrm{ZPP}^{\mathrm{NP}}\). An argument of Sengupta gave the symmetric alternation class \(\mathrm{S_2^P}\). Chakaravarthy and Roy then gave two collapses: to \(\mathrm{O_2^P}\), the input-oblivious version of \(\mathrm{S_2^P}\), in which certificates may depend on the input length but not on the input; and to \(\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{prMA}}\). The two looked incomparable. Hirsch and Volkovich had already united them with a collapse to \(\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{prOMA}}\), and with Williams went slightly lower.

The new paper [arXiv:2611.16096] counts three deepest collapse classes, pairwise incomparable as far as anyone knows. The first is \(\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{prOMA}}\) and its variants. The second is \(\mathrm{ZPP}^{\mathrm{ONP}}\), the input-oblivious form of Köbler–Watanabe. The third is \(\mathrm{OMIN} = \mathrm{OL_2^P}\), the oblivious version of the Korten–Pitassi class in which acceptance is read off the minimum of a polynomial-time decidable linear order. The collapse to \(\mathrm{OMIN}\) is itself new here. It matters because \(\mathrm{OMIN}\) is already known to have fixed-polynomial circuit lower bounds, and no smaller collapse class with that property is known.

The smaller the collapse class, the stronger the lower bound that falls out of it.

The main theorem defines a class \(\mathrm{OM}\), shows it sits inside all three, and shows that \(\mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{P/poly}\) implies \(\mathrm{PH} = \mathrm{OM}\). The collapse holds even when the certified advice string is required to be unique.

The authors add their own caution. A Karp–Lipton class inside all three is easy to build by a closure construction over tally oracles. Their claim is that \(\mathrm{OM}\) comes from a natural model of computation and is, for instance, closed under polynomial-time Turing reductions. Naturalness is an argument rather than a theorem, and the paper presents it as one.

Generator, tester, decoder

The model has three parts. A polynomial-time generator sees only \(1^n\) and, with an oracle, writes down an advice string. A tester, also blind to the input, must accept everything the generator can legally output and nothing that would mislead the decoder. A polynomial-time decoder then reads the actual input and the advice, and decides. In \(\mathrm{OM}\) the generator's oracle is a strict oblivious Merlin–Arthur class, the tester is in \(\mathrm{co}\)-\(\mathrm{ONP}\), and the decoder is in \(\mathrm{P}\). The tester's job rests on a simple observation: the minimum of an oblivious linear order is certified by the statement that nothing is smaller, and the minimum itself refutes every rival.

The three containments come from running the same generator three ways. Run it with its oracle and the class lands in the Merlin–Arthur collapse class. Replace the oracle by a fixed language in \(\mathrm{L_2^P}\) and the advice becomes canonical, which places the class in \(\mathrm{OMIN}\). Sample the oracle's answers at random and let the tester check the result, and the class lands in \(\mathrm{ZPP}^{\mathrm{ONP}}\).

The upper end

The second result bounds the whole picture from above. The paper proves \(\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{prS_2^P}} \subseteq \mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{prSBP}}\), where a \(\mathrm{prSBP}\) oracle amounts to approximate counting and \(\mathrm{SBP}\) lies between \(\mathrm{MA}\) and \(\mathrm{AM}\). This strengthens the Chakaravarthy–Roy containment \(\mathrm{S_2^P} \subseteq \mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{prAM}}\). The tool is a version of the Goldreich–Wigderson lemma: every Boolean matrix has a small set of rows that together beat every column, or a small set of columns that together beat every row. Chakaravarthy and Roy found such sets with an \(\mathrm{AM}\)-type oracle. Here approximate counts decide, round by round, whether to pick a row or a column, choosing the denser side of what remains.

Two consequences follow. Every language in \(\mathrm{O_2^P}\) has polynomial-size circuits constructible in \(\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{prSBP}}\). And if approximate counting can be done in \(\mathrm{FP}^{\mathrm{NP}}\), then \(\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{prS_2^P}} = \mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{NP}}\).

What it does not show

The win–win does not yet reach \(\mathrm{OM}\). Input-oblivious classes lie in \(\mathrm{P/poly}\) by definition, so they cannot contain \(\mathrm{NP}\) unless \(\mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{P/poly}\) already holds. For them the collapse and the lower bound are separate problems. For \(\mathrm{O_2^P}\) the lower bound came long after the collapse, through range avoidance. For \(\mathrm{OM}\) it is open.

For a reader who follows circuit lower bounds, the result settles the location of the floor more than it moves the bounds. Every lower bound obtained this way remains fixed-polynomial. Whether \(\mathrm{ONP} \subseteq \mathrm{OL_2^P}\) holds unconditionally is open. So is whether \(\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{prSBP}}\) contains \(\mathrm{ZPP}^{\mathrm{NP}}\). The collapse is now as deep as anyone can currently make it. Whether it buys a lower bound is the next question.

THE LONG VIEW

The polynomial method reaches AC0[2]-Frege

Alekseev, Gryaznov, Itsykson, Pitassi, Robere and Tzameret prove exponential lower bounds for semantic \(\Sigma\Pi\Sigma\)-PC over \(\mathbb{F}_2\), superpolynomial bounds for semantic \(\Sigma\Pi\Sigma\Pi\)-PC (including the first superpolynomial Res(log) bound for the weak pigeonhole principle), and, for each fixed \(d \ge 2\), depth-\(d\) formulas that are hard for semantic depth-\(d\) \(\mathrm{AC}^0[2]\)-Frege but easy at depth \(d+1\); the result caps nearly forty years of attempts to carry the Razborov–Smolensky method from circuits to proofs.

The pigeonhole as a test case

Propositional proof complexity took its first hard instance from counting. In 1985 Haken showed that resolution needs size \(\exp(\Omega(n))\) to refute the pigeonhole principle. Razborov's survey records the common view that this result started the area [S1]. In 1987 Buss showed that the same principle has polynomial-size Frege proofs, so it can only separate weaker systems [S1].

In 1988 Ajtai obtained the first superpolynomial bound for bounded-depth Frege, using non-standard models of arithmetic [S1]. Bellantoni, Pitassi and Urquhart made the argument combinatorial in 1992, with a bound of \(n^{\epsilon_d \log n}\). Pitassi, Beame and Impagliazzo, and independently Krajíček, Pudlák and Woods, then raised it to \(\exp(n^{\epsilon_d})\) [S1].

Circuits with parity gates

In 1987 Razborov and Smolensky proved exponential lower bounds for constant-depth circuits with AND, OR, NOT and mod-\(p\) gates [S2]. Smolensky's algebraic version shows that \(\mathrm{AC}^0[p]\) cannot compute \(\mathrm{MOD}_q\) for \(q \ne p\) [S3]. The method approximates each gate by a random low-degree polynomial over a finite field. Over \(\mathbb{F}_2\), parity gates cost nothing, because addition already is parity.

The proof-system analogue, \(\mathrm{AC}^0[p]\)-Frege, became a target soon after. In 1996 Buss, Impagliazzo, Krajíček, Pudlák, Razborov and Sgall connected algebraic proof systems with bounded-depth Frege with modular counting. This week's paper describes the transfer of Razborov–Smolensky to proofs as “a significant challenge since the 1990s” [S4]. The circuit bound has stood for nearly four decades. The proof bound did not follow from it.

Polynomial calculus and degree

In 1996 Clegg, Edmonds and Impagliazzo introduced a Gröbner-basis proof system, now called polynomial calculus (PC) [S4]. In 1998 Razborov proved that every PC proof of the functional pigeonhole principle needs degree \(\Omega(n)\), over any field [S5]. Impagliazzo, Pudlák and Sgall simplified the argument in 1999 [S1]. Alekhnovich and Razborov gave a general route to degree lower bounds in 2001–03, which Mikša and Nordström later generalised [S4].

A common tool in this work is the pseudo-reduction operator. This is a linear map on polynomials of degree at most \(B\) with two properties: it sends every axiom to zero, and its kernel is closed under multiplication by a variable while the degree stays within \(B\). If the operator does not send \(1\) to zero, no refutation of degree \(B\) exists [S4]. The machinery produced bounds on degree. It did not produce bounds on size.

The weak pigeonhole principle

With \(2n\) pigeons and \(n\) holes, the principle is much harder to prove hard. In 1988 Buss and Turán showed that resolution needs \(\exp(\Omega(n))\) for the onto-functional version. Atserias, Bonet and Esteban extended this to Res(2) [S1].

On the other side, in 2002 Maciel, Pitassi and Woods gave quasipolynomial \(n^{O(\log n)}\) proofs in resolution over polylogarithmic-width DNFs [S1]. These yield quasipolynomial Res(log) refutations [S4]. The same year, Segerlind, Buss and Impagliazzo proved a switching lemma for small restrictions. It gave exponential bounds in Res(\(k\)) for \(k \le \sqrt{\log n/\log\log n}\) [S6]. In 2015 Razborov removed the square root, reaching \(k = \epsilon\log n/\log\log n\) [S7]. Width \(\log n\) stayed out of reach.

Resolution over parities

In 2008 Raz and Tzameret introduced resolution over linear equations [S4]. Itsykson and Sokolov studied its mod-2 version, Res(⊕), and proved lower bounds for the tree-like form [S8]. Further restricted forms followed:

  • Regular Res(⊕): Efremenko, Garlík and Itsykson, 2023 [S9].
  • Bounded-depth Res(⊕): Alekseev and Itsykson, by lifting, 2025 [S10].
  • Polynomial-depth Res(⊕): Alekseev and Gaevoy, January 2026 [S10].

In September 2026 Braun proved the first lower bound for unrestricted DAG-like Res(⊕). Refutations of the bit pigeonhole principle need \(2^{\Omega(n/\log^2 n)}\) clauses [S10]. The proof is formalised in Lean 4 and, in Braun's words, was “developed with substantial AI assistance” [S10].

In October two related results appeared. Pang gave a degree–size relation for Res(PC) over prime fields. Conneryd used it to prove \(\exp(\Omega_p((\log n)^{3/2}))\) bounds for syntactic depth-2 \(\mathrm{AC}^0[p]\)-Frege on random \((\log n)\)-CNFs [S4, S11].

Two pieces already in place

Two earlier results set up the target.

The first is Grochow and Pitassi's Ideal Proof System (IPS) of 2018. It converts depth-\(d\) \(\mathrm{AC}^0[2]\)-Frege refutations into \(\mathbb{F}_2\)-IPS refutations of polynomially related size and depth \(O(d)\) [S4]. In 2024 Hakoniemi, Limaye and Tzameret obtained constant-depth IPS lower bounds. These hold only for individual degree \(O(\log\log n)\), which is where the Frege simulation breaks [S4].

The second is a construction for the coin problem. Limaye, Sreenivasaiah, Srinivasan, Tripathi and Venkitesh built monotone \(\mathrm{AC}^0\) formulas for the \(\delta\)-coin problem, of depth \(d\) and size \(\exp(O(d(1/\delta)^{1/(d-1)}))\). They used these to prove a fixed-depth size hierarchy for \(\mathrm{AC}^0[\oplus]\) formulas [S12].

This week

Alekseev, Gryaznov, Itsykson, Pitassi, Robere and Tzameret [ECCC:TR26-316] read Braun's argument as an implicit annihilating-polynomial argument. They recast it in terms of pseudo-reduction operators [S4].

The argument runs as follows. Multiply every line of a semantic refutation by a low-degree polynomial \(f\) that vanishes on the support of each high-degree term. The refutation becomes a low-degree derivation of \(f\). If the operator \(R\) satisfies \(R(f) \ne 0\), that derivation cannot exist. Ruling out low degree needs only \(R \ne 0\). Ruling out small size needs \(R\) to have large rank, and the bound is \(S(\pi) \ge \mathrm{rank}(R_{\le k})/H_{n-r}(k)\) [S4].

Three further tools extend this [S4]:

  • Razborov–Smolensky approximation carries the argument to depth four.
  • Lifting and random restrictions remove the bound on bottom fan-in.
  • Coin-problem formulas supply an approximate-majority gadget for \(\mathrm{AC}^0[2]\)-Frege, where the bound is \(\exp(\Omega_d((\log N)^{d/(d-1)}))\).

The results are these:

  • \(2^{\Omega(n)}\) for semantic \(\Sigma\Pi\Sigma\)-PC on the bit and functional pigeonhole principles and on random 3-CNFs.
  • \(\exp(\Omega((\log n)^2/\log\log n))\) for \(\Sigma\Pi\Sigma\Pi\)-PC on \(\mathrm{PHP}^{2n}_n\), which settles the Res(log) question for the weak pigeonhole principle.
  • For each fixed \(d \ge 2\), depth-\(d\) formulas that are hard for semantic depth-\(d\) \(\mathrm{AC}^0[2]\)-Frege but easy at depth \(d+1\).

The authors state that they used OpenAI models as interactive tools, and that they wrote and verified the paper themselves [S4].

What it does not show

The hard instances are deep formulas, not CNFs. There is a reason: depth-5 semantic \(\Sigma\Pi\Sigma\Pi\Sigma\)-PC refutes every unsatisfiable CNF in polynomial size [S4]. Syntactic \(\mathrm{AC}^0[p]\)-Frege lower bounds for CNFs remain open [S13]. The route through Pang's condition needs near-maximal PC degree bounds, which the authors call “a form of SETH for PC” [S4]. The work covers \(\mathbb{F}_2\) only. In Res(log), the upper and lower bounds for the weak pigeonhole principle are now both quasipolynomial, but their exponents do not meet [S4].

The technique: Razborov–Smolensky polynomial approximation

The method replaces each AND or OR gate of a constant-depth circuit by a random low-degree polynomial over \(\mathbb{F}_2\). On any fixed input, this polynomial agrees with the gate with high probability. Parity gates need no approximation, since addition over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) is parity. Applied to a whole circuit, the method yields one low-degree polynomial that is correct on most inputs. A lower bound then follows by showing the target function has no such approximation.

OR of \(n\) bits. Choose random subsets \(S_1, \dots, S_k \subseteq [n]\) and set

\(p(x) = 1 + \prod_{j=1}^{k}\big(1 + \sum_{i \in S_j} x_i\big)\).

This polynomial has three properties:

  • Its degree is at most \(k\).
  • At \(x = 0\), every subset sum is \(0\), so \(p(0) = 0\), which is correct.
  • For a fixed nonzero \(x\), each subset sum equals \(1\) with probability exactly \(1/2\). So \(p\) is wrong only if all \(k\) sums vanish, which happens with probability \(2^{-k}\).

A toy instance. Take \(n = 3\), \(k = 2\), \(S_1 = \{1,2\}\) and \(S_2 = \{2,3\}\). Then \(p = 1 + (1+x_1+x_2)(1+x_2+x_3)\). Multilinearising (\(x_i^2 = x_i\)) and reducing mod 2 gives

\(p = x_1+x_2+x_3+x_1x_2+x_1x_3+x_2x_3\).

This agrees with OR on seven of the eight inputs. It fails only at \(111\), where both subset sums are even.

Whole circuits. Approximate the gates from the bottom up. Each AND or OR layer multiplies the degree by at most \(k\), so the degree grows to about \(k^d\) at depth \(d\). A union bound over \(s\) gates gives error at most \(s \cdot 2^{-k}\) on each fixed input.

How the new paper uses it. A random error bound is not enough for a proof, because the approximation must hold everywhere. The new paper therefore asks for one polynomial \(f\) that annihilates the difference between every term and its approximation. This makes the approximations exact along the entire refutation.

The late column. The week's papers set against each other, looking for the pattern nobody wrote down. Plain words. No favourites.

The week had no headline of its own. It had one from late October, and a stack of papers doing the accounting.

That headline is the pair of subexponential 3-SAT algorithms reported by OpenAI and Anthropic, and with them the refutation of ETH. Separately, Alman and Vassilevska Williams have refuted 3SUM and APSP. Nobody this week re-proves any of it. The papers ask what it touches.

Start with the direct consequence. Strassle and Subercaseaux [arXiv:2611.11832] show the algorithms imply \(\mathsf{NTIME}[t]\subseteq\mathsf{DTIME}[2^{O(t\log\log t/\sqrt{\log t})}]\), and \(2^{O(t/\sqrt{\log t})}\) from a stronger consequence of the proof. The route is a generalisation of Cook–Levin. Any subexponential circuit-CSP algorithm transfers to \(\mathsf{NTIME}\) with the same savings. That is the honest size of the thing: a \(\sqrt{\log t}\) in the exponent.

Szeider [zenodo:24243988] takes three statements of the Anthropic preprint as given. From them he derives \(2^{o(n)}\) smooth decomposable arithmetic circuits for the weighted model count of every 3-CNF. Hence \(2^{o(n)}\) IPS refutations of every unsatisfiable 3-CNF, over every field of characteristic other than 2. Lean checks the derivation from the three statements. It does not check the three statements. The note says plainly that both algorithms are galactic and that it establishes no improvement over exhaustive search at physical sizes.

Then the repairs. Bilyk, Munduzbaev, Sharma and Siam [arXiv:2611.12321] settle the Gaspers–Szeider question: strong backdoors to forests in \(2^{2^{O(k)}}|\varphi|\) time on general non-tautological CNFs, with W[1]-hardness for treewidth \(t\ge 2\). Their tightness argument for the double exponential no longer rests on ETH. It rests on 3-SAT having no \(2^{o(N/\log N)}\) algorithm, a bar placed below the claimed ones on purpose. Karthik and Minzer [arXiv:2611.14892] repair a \(k\)-means reduction whose hypothesis, XXH, does not hold in the regime where it was used, and rebuild it on Almost ETH. Cheng, Huang and Wu [arXiv:2611.17690] prove read-\(k\) \(\mathsf{BPL}=\mathsf{BPL}\) for constant \(k\), and note what follows if the announced \(\mathsf{BPL}=\mathsf{L}\) is true.

Tomar [hexagon:2611.00388v1] does the job for 3SUM and APSP. When a load-bearing wall comes out, someone has to walk the building and check which ceilings are still up. Most set-disjointness, dynamic-graph and triangle bounds survive, because their reductions produce balanced instances, or lopsided ones with a middle part too large for the new algorithms. A Goldstein–Lewenstein–Porat bound for Set Disjointness over small universes is false. Some Kopelowitz–Pettie–Porat trade-offs have lost their support. And the surviving hypotheses drew their evidence largely from the refuted ones. For several bounds the hypothesis is the bound itself, restated for the instances that remain.

The ceilings are up. Nobody has checked what holds them.

So the pattern. The conditional lower bound was for a long time the cheapest instrument the field owned. This week it acquired a price. Hypotheses are being weakened by hand to sit just under whatever was claimed last. The premises downstream are no longer conjectures about the world. They are statements lifted from a preprint which, by Tucker's account [zenodo:24227146], Anthropic itself says no person is known to have checked.

The other half of the week runs the other way. Alekseev, Gryaznov, Itsykson, Pitassi, Robere and Tzameret [ECCC:TR26-316] prove unconditional lower bounds: \(2^{\Omega(n)}\) for random 3-CNFs against semantic \(\Sigma\Pi\Sigma\)-PC over \(\mathbb{F}_2\); superpolynomial bounds for semantic \(\Sigma\Pi\Sigma\Pi\)-PC that settle Res(log) for the weak pigeonhole principle; and the first unconditional hard instances for \(AC^0[2]\)-Frege at every fixed depth, using deep formulas. The method is Braun's AI-generated argument for resolution over parities, distilled into a framework of pseudo-reduction operators. Dvir [arXiv:2611.13281] writes up constant-query LCCs over the reals, found by ChatGPT Astra, previously conjectured not to exist.

Set the two proof-complexity papers side by side. Lower bounds for random 3-CNFs climb from below, in characteristic 2. A conditional subexponential upper bound sits at IPS, in characteristic other than 2. Szeider lists where such an upper bound is ruled out: resolution, Res(\(k\)) for fixed \(k\), polynomial calculus, sum-of-squares. The interval between is where proof complexity now works, and both of this week's markers on it trace back, in part, to machine output.

Only one of the two needs re-inspection if a preprint changes.
