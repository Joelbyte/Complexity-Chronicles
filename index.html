FINE-GRAINED COMPLEXITY

The hyperclique conjecture falls

For every fixed \(k\) there is an \(\varepsilon_k > 0\) such that \(k\)-hypercliques in an \(n\)-vertex \((k-1)\)-uniform hypergraph can be counted in \(O(n^{k-\varepsilon_k})\) time, unconditionally; as corollaries, Max-\(k\)SAT runs in \(2^{(1-\varepsilon)n}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(m,n)\) time and length-\(k\) arithmetic progressions are detected in \(n^{2-\varepsilon}\) time, with \(\varepsilon\) depending on \(k\).

Alman, Li, Pant, Pratt and Williams [arXiv:2611.05082] count the \(k\)-hypercliques of a \((k-1)\)-uniform hypergraph on \(n\) vertices in \(O(n^{k-\varepsilon_k})\) time, for every fixed \(k\) and some \(\varepsilon_k > 0\). Brute force takes \(n^k\). The hyperclique conjecture of Lincoln, Vassilevska Williams and Williams (SODA 2018) said that no polynomial saving of this kind was possible. It is refuted, and no assumption is needed.

The method is algebraic. The authors build a uniform family of arithmetic circuits of size \(O(n^{k-\varepsilon_k})\) for the \(k\)-hyperclique tensor, the higher-order generalisation of the matrix multiplication tensor. When \(k = 3\) the hypergraph is an ordinary graph, the hypercliques are triangles, and fast matrix multiplication has long supplied the saving. For larger \(k\) there was no such tool. The conjecture was, in effect, a bet that none existed.

Two corollaries follow. For every \(k\) there is an \(\varepsilon > 0\) such that Max-\(k\)SAT can be solved in \(2^{(1-\varepsilon)n} \cdot \mathrm{poly}(m,n)\) time. And for every \(k\), arithmetic progressions of length \(k\) in subsets of \(\{0,1,\ldots,n\}\) can be detected in \(n^{2-\varepsilon}\) time. The authors state that both corollaries refute other prominent conjectures in the literature.

What it does not show is just as clear. The saving \(\varepsilon\) depends on \(k\), so the Max-\(k\)SAT result does not touch SETH, which asks for a saving that holds uniformly as the clause width grows. The abstract gives no value for \(\varepsilon_k\). Until one is computed, the improvement is a fact about exponents rather than about running times.

A perspective inside traces the split-and-list route from Max-2SAT to hypercliques.

[arXiv:2611.05082] Finding Hypercliques Faster Than Brute Force (Josh Alman, Baitian Li, Neha Pant, Kevin Pratt et al.)

FINE-GRAINED COMPLEXITY

k-Clique conjecture refuted, even if ω = 2

Vassilevska Williams and Zamir count weighted \(k\)-cliques in \(n\)-vertex graphs in \(n^{(\omega/3-c)k}\) time for every sufficiently large \(k\), with \(c > 0\) independent of both \(\omega\) and \(k\), refuting the \(k\)-Clique conjecture whatever the value of \(\omega\).

The \(k\)-Clique conjecture held that the matrix-multiplication approach, at roughly \(n^{\omega k/3}\) time, is essentially optimal for finding a \(k\)-clique. Vassilevska Williams and Zamir [arXiv:2611.09764] beat it by a constant \(c\) in the coefficient of \(k\), for every large enough \(k\). Because \(c\) does not depend on \(\omega\), the refutation holds even if \(\omega = 2\). The algorithm solves the more general problem of counting weighted \(k\)-cliques.

Any conditional lower bound obtained by reduction from \(k\)-Clique must now find another source of hardness or settle for a weaker statement. Read beside the lead story, the week has removed two of the field's standard assumptions.

The abstract states neither the value of \(c\) nor how large \(k\) must be.

[arXiv:2611.09764] The k-Clique hypothesis is dead, for good (Virginia Vassilevska Williams, Or Zamir)

BOOLEAN FUNCTIONS

Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture claimed proved

Turturean claims that every \(f\colon\{-1,1\}^n\to[-1,1]\) of degree at most \(d\) has a variable of influence at least \(c \mathrm{Var}(f)^{5/2}/d^6\), which by Aaronson and Ambainis would mean the acceptance probability of any \(T\)-query quantum algorithm can be approximated on most inputs by a classical algorithm making \(\mathrm{poly}(T)\) queries.

Aaronson and Ambainis conjectured that every bounded low-degree polynomial on the discrete cube has an influential variable. Turturean [arXiv:2611.09773] claims a proof, with an explicit bound: influence at least \(c \mathrm{Var}(f)^{5/2}/d^{6}\) for an absolute constant \(c > 0\). In the original formulation, for \([0,1]\)-valued polynomials on \(\{0,1\}^n\) with variance at least \(\varepsilon\), this gives a variable of influence at least \((\varepsilon/d)^7\).

The consequence was shown by Aaronson and Ambainis themselves: quantum query algorithms can be simulated classically, on most inputs, with polynomially many queries. Superpolynomial quantum speedups in the query model would then require structure, such as a promise on the inputs. The author describes the proof as elementary, with steps inspired by the work of others.

The paper is 60 pages, has a single author and comes with a Lean formalisation. A formalisation is only as strong as its statement, and the first check will be that the formal theorem is the one in the title.

[arXiv:2611.09773] A proof of the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture (David Turturean)

CIRCUIT LOWER BOUNDS

Two lower bounds, one of them true

Unconditionally, either \(\mathrm{NE}\) contains a language requiring Boolean circuits of size at least \(2^n/(2n)\), which implies \(\mathrm{NP}\not\subseteq\mathrm{SIZE}[n^k]\) for every constant \(k\), or the constant-free Valiant conjecture \(\mathrm{VP}^0_{\mathbb{Z}} \ne \mathrm{VNP}^0_{\mathbb{Z}}\) holds.

Kabanets and Impagliazzo (2004) showed that derandomising polynomial identity testing yields circuit lower bounds. Chukhin, Gajulapalli, Golovnev and Mihajlin [arXiv:2611.07972] give a fine-grained version and observe that the particular identity tests the argument needs can be run over the integers without any derandomisation assumption. The tool is a deterministic PIT algorithm for syntactically set-multilinear circuits running in \(s^{O(1)}t^{5d/6}\) time. The result is a disjunction that holds outright: a \(2^n/(2n)\) Boolean lower bound in \(\mathrm{NE}\), or \(\mathrm{VP}^0_{\mathbb{Z}} \ne \mathrm{VNP}^0_{\mathbb{Z}}\).

Over a fixed finite field they give deterministic \(O(st^{d-1})\)-time PIT for size-\(s\) circuits computing degree-\(d\) polynomials in \(t\) variables. Improving this by a factor of \(t^{\varepsilon}\), even nondeterministically, would give either the same Boolean bound or a \(t^{1+\varepsilon/2}\) arithmetic lower bound for a \(\mathrm{VNP}\) family. Combined with range avoidance, an \(\mathrm{NP}\) algorithm for PIT over \(\mathbb{Z}\) would give a purely arithmetic win-win.

The theorem does not say which alternative holds. Nor does it say how one would find out.

[arXiv:2611.07972] A Little Derandomization Goes a Long Way: Unconditional Circuit Lower Bounds using Low-Degree PIT (Nikolai Chukhin, Karthik Gajulapalli, Alexander Golovnev, Ivan Mihajlin)

Fine-grained complexity

After ETH, what holds the lower bounds up

Taking the claimed refutation of ETH as given, the MIT Hardness Group prove that Stearns and Hunt's 1990 Satisfiability Hypothesis is equivalent to Weak ETH, and that it preserves reduction-based, parameterised, gap and counting lower bounds up to an arbitrarily small loss in the exponent. Separately, Chukhin proves without any hypothesis that constant-depth circuits for \(k\)-OV in polylogarithmic dimension need \(n^{k-\varepsilon}\) gates.

Two papers this week answer the same question. If ETH has fallen, what do the field's hardness results stand on? [arXiv:2611.02650] answers with an older hypothesis. [arXiv:2611.06231] answers with no hypothesis at all, in a model weak enough to be analysed.

The background is the pair of claimed algorithms from OpenAI and Anthropic, which count the satisfying assignments of a \(k\)-CNF in time \((n+m)^{O(1)}2^{O(n\log\log n/\sqrt{\log n})}\). Both papers take these as refuting ETH and SETH. Neither paper's own theorems depend on those algorithms being correct.

Stearns and Hunt, again

The claimed exponent is \(o(n)\), which kills ETH. It is also \(n^{1-o(1)}\), which does not touch the Satisfiability Hypothesis (SH) that Stearns and Hunt stated in 1990: CNF SAT on inputs of length \(L\) has no \(O(2^{L^{1-\varepsilon}})\)-time algorithm for any constant \(\varepsilon>0\).

One answer leans on a hypothesis from 1990. The other leans on nothing, in a model small enough to be analysed.

Brunner, Demaine, Frei, Gomez, Hecher, Li and Lynch, writing as the MIT Hardness Group, prove SH equivalent to Weak ETH, which forbids \(2^{n^{1-\varepsilon}}(n+m)^{O(1)}\)-time algorithms for 3SAT on \(n\) variables. It is also equivalent to the \(k\)SAT version for each fixed \(k\ge 3\). The counting versions, #SH and Weak #ETH, are equivalent to each other, and so are the bounded-error randomised versions.

The proof has two steps. A parsimonious Tseitin encoding relates input length to the formula size \(n+m\). Sparsification then relates size to variable count. The usual ETH argument uses a fixed sparsification parameter, which here costs too much. Instead the parameter shrinks with \(n\), as \(n^{-\delta}\). This produces fewer branches but denser formulas, and \(\delta\) is chosen small enough that both exponents stay below one.

What survives, and at what price

The main result is a transfer rule. Take a reduction from 3SAT of size \(s\) to an instance of size \(N\le s^{b+o(1)}\). Under SH, it rules out \(2^{O(N^{1/b-\varepsilon})}\)-time algorithms for every \(\varepsilon>0\), and the same holds with a parameter \(k\) in place of \(N\).

The paper's table applies the rule to known reductions. Vertex Cover, Independent Set and 3-Colouring have no \(2^{O(n^{1-\varepsilon})}\) algorithms. Planar Hamiltonian Cycle has none in \(2^{O(n^{1/2-\varepsilon})}\). Graph Homomorphism, previously excluded at \(2^{o(n\log n)}\) under ETH, is now excluded only at \(2^{O(n^{1-\varepsilon})}\). Constant-gap hardness for Max-3SAT is rederived through Dinur's nearly linear PCP. Under #SH, #2SAT has no \(O(2^{(n+m)^{1-\varepsilon}})\) algorithm, even on formulas of constant degree.

The price is precision. The \(\varepsilon\) swallows logarithmic factors in the exponent, so \(2^{o(N)}\) and \(2^{o(N\log N)}\) bounds collapse into one. The \(2^{o(k\log k)}\) bound for \(k\times k\) Clique is lost. The paper points to a separate \(k\times k\) Clique Hypothesis, proposed by Szeider, for anyone who needs it back. Tight bounds of the Graph Homomorphism kind, stated to a logarithm in the exponent, now need more than SAT hardness alone. SH forbids saving a polynomial factor in the exponent, and the claimed algorithms save only a polylogarithmic one; that is the room the field now works in.

No hypothesis at all

Chukhin takes the other route. Exhaustive search for a hyperclique on \(\ell\) vertices, in an \(r\)-uniform hypergraph on \(m\) vertices, is a depth-two circuit with at most \(m^\ell\) gates. Amano showed in 2010 that this is almost optimal in constant depth, but only for constant \(\ell\). Chukhin extends the bound to hypercliques of polynomial size. Depth-\(h\) circuits need \(m^{\Omega(\ell)}\) gates for \(\ell\le m^{1/h-\varepsilon}\). When \(\ell=m^{o(1)}\) tends to infinity they need \(m^{(c_r-o(1))\ell}\) gates, with \(c_r\) tending to one as \(r\) grows.

Three consequences follow. Rossman's \(n^{k/4}\) bound for \(k\)-Clique, known for constant \(k\), extends to \(n^{\Omega(k)}\) gates for \(k\le n^{1/(h-1)-\varepsilon}\). Through a reduction of Choudhury, Limaye, Sreenivasaiah and Srinivasan, \(k\)-OV in dimension \((\log n)^{O(1)}\) needs \(n^{k-\varepsilon}\) gates at every fixed depth. That proves the \(k\)-OV Conjecture for constant-depth circuits in polylogarithmic dimension, where the earlier bound needed dimension \(n^\varepsilon\). A separate, simpler argument shows that depth-three circuits for 2-OV in dimension \((1+\eta)\log n\) need \(\Omega(n^2)\) wires, which answers a question of Kane and Williams.

The method plants a hyperclique in a dense random hypergraph. Earlier work planted into a sparse one and used the switching lemma, which fails once the edge probability tends to one. Here each gate is read as a multilinear polynomial. The circuit must eventually build a monomial touching every planted vertex, so some gate must multiply two smaller pieces into a larger one. Tal's Fourier tail bound for constant-depth circuits shows that this is unlikely at any fixed gate, because the gate cannot know where the planted set lies.

What remains open

SH itself is a conjecture, and the paper names fallbacks should it fail, down to ruling out quasipolynomial time. Chukhin's bounds hold only in constant depth. Against circuits of arbitrary depth, an \(n^{\Omega(k)}\) bound for \(k\)-Clique with \(k=\omega(1)\) would give \(\mathrm{NP}\not\subseteq\mathrm{P/poly}\). The general \(k\)-OV Conjecture remains open, even for \(k=2\). The paper also notes that the claimed SAT algorithms are not known to give \(f(k)n^{o(k)}\) algorithms for \(k\)-Clique, or \(n^{k-\varepsilon}\mathrm{poly}(d)\) algorithms for \(k\)-OV.

Elsewhere this week, [arXiv:2611.05082] refutes the hyperclique conjecture with arithmetic circuits beating brute force. The two results are consistent. One concerns constant-depth Boolean circuits, and an algorithm built from arithmetic circuits need not fit that model.

Promise CSPs

Mod-\(p\) equations: four colours easy, three hard

Nakajima, Verwimp and Živný classify the promise problem of colouring the variables of a system \(x+y+z\equiv 0 \pmod p\), promised to have a solution that is nonconstant on every equation, so that no equation is monochromatic. For primes \(p\ge 5\) the problem is polynomial-time solvable with \(c\ge 4\) colours and NP-hard with \(c=3\), and the same holds for every finite Abelian group without elements of order 2 or 3. The tractable side comes from a new reduction built on Abelian embeddings.

The problem

Take a system of equations, each of the form \(x+y+z\equiv 0 \pmod p\). Suppose it is promised that some assignment satisfies every equation without giving all three variables of any equation the same value. For \(p\ne 3\) this simply rules out \((0,0,0)\). The task is weaker than finding that assignment: colour the variables with \(c\) colours so that no equation is monochromatic. In promise CSP notation this is \(\mathrm{PCSP}(\mathrm{Eqn}^*(\mathbb{Z}_p),\mathrm{NAE}_c)\) [arXiv:2611.04569].

The linear system alone is solved by Gaussian elimination. The ban on constant equations is what makes the exact problem NP-hard. Not-all-equal on \(c\) colours is the weakest target that still excludes constant tuples, so the question is whether the relation is promise-useful at all, and with how many colours.

Two cases were already settled. For \(p=2\) and \(c=2\) the problem is, after complementation, 1-in-3 versus NAE-3-SAT, which Brakensiek and Guruswami showed tractable. For \(p=3\) it is 3-rainbow versus \(c\)-colouring of 3-uniform hypergraphs, for which McDiarmid gave a randomised algorithm; deterministic algorithms for all \(c\) follow from the \(p=c=2\) case.

A reconfigurable relation admits no Abelian embedding, and the detrivialised equation relations have no single-coordinate moves at all.

The result

For every prime \(p\ge 5\) the classification is complete. With \(c\ge 4\) colours the search problem is solvable in polynomial time. With \(c=3\) even the decision version is NP-hard. With \(c=2\) the problem is not well defined, because some instances satisfy both the yes and the no condition. The theorem extends to every nontrivial finite Abelian group with no elements of order 2 or 3.

The four-colour algorithm is not captured by the standard tools. The authors show that neither \(k\)-consistency, for any fixed \(k\), nor the combined BLP+AIP relaxation solves it. It is a singleton algorithm in Zhuk's sense.

The idea of the algorithm

An Abelian embedding, a notion Bhangale, Khot and Minzer introduced for quantitative approximation of satisfiable CSPs, maps the values of a relation into an Abelian group so that, coordinate by coordinate, every tuple of the relation sums to zero, and not trivially. The second ingredient is a \(c\)-rounding: a colouring of the nonzero group elements such that, in any nonzero solution of \(g_1+g_2+g_3=0\), the nonzero entries do not all share a colour. \(\mathbb{Z}_p\) has a 4-rounding for \(p\ge 5\). \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) and \(\mathbb{Z}_3\) have none, because of \(1+1+0=0\) and \(1+1+1=0\).

The algorithm repeatedly solves the linear system for a solution in which some variable is nonzero, colours every nonzero variable by the rounding, and deletes those variables with every constraint they touch. Any constraint touched for the first time has two or three nonzero entries summing to zero, so it is already nonmonochromatic whatever happens later. If no nonzero solution exists, the promised solution must map entirely to zero, and the instance reduces to the subrelation sitting over zero. For \(\mathrm{Eqn}^*(\mathbb{Z}_p)\) that subrelation is empty, so the procedure finishes. In general it gives a polynomial-time reduction from \(\mathrm{PCSP}(R,\mathrm{NAE}_c)\) to the same problem with a strengthened promise. The same framework recovers the Brakensiek–Guruswami algorithm, through an embedding of 1-in-3 into \(\mathbb{Z}\) with the sign as a 2-rounding. The authors also show the reduction can be carried out by constraint singleton AIP, and characterise such reductions algebraically along the lines of Dalmau and Opršal.

The hardness side

Recent conditional hardness, by Banakh, Barsukov and Nakajima under the Rich 2-to-1 conjecture, covers symmetric reconfigurable relations: those whose tuples are connected by single-coordinate changes. For symmetric relations the two regimes are disjoint. A reconfigurable relation admits no Abelian embedding, and the detrivialised equation relations have no single-coordinate moves at all. The three-colour hardness therefore needs a different route, and it is unconditional.

That route is an additive-combinatorial rigidity theorem. Call a partition of \(\mathbb{Z}_p\) into three parts valid if \((0,0,0)\) is the only solution of \(x+y+z\equiv 0\) lying inside one part. For every prime \(p\ge 7\) there is exactly one valid partition up to scaling and relabelling: cut \(\{0,\ldots,p-1\}\) into thirds. The proof uses the Cauchy–Davenport and Vosper theorems. For \(p=5\) there are two, and that case is handled separately, by a minion homomorphism to a promise problem on an unbalanced directed cycle already known to be NP-hard.

What remains open

The authors name the complexity of \(\mathrm{PCSP}(\mathrm{Eqn}^*(\mathbb{Z}_2^n),\mathrm{NAE}_c)\) for \(n\ge 2\) and large \(c\) as an open question. These relations have Abelian embeddings, but not of a kind the framework can use. The tractability argument extends to equations of arity \(r\), with more colours, but no matching hardness is claimed there.

The broader question is how far Abelian embeddings explain promise-usefulness in general. Concurrent work of Chan and Ng also gives such embeddings an algorithmic role, on the quantitative side. One classified family is evidence, not a theory. It is, though, a family that the usual relaxations miss and that the reconfiguration-based hardness cannot reach, and both facts are now on record.

[arXiv:2611.04569] Abelian embeddings make useful promises: approximation of detrivialised equations (Tamio-Vesa Nakajima, Zephyr Verwimp, Stanislav Živný)

Proof complexity

Resolution lower bounds now provable in constant depth

Arteche, de Rezende, Khaniki and Risse show that a satisfying assignment can be extracted from a Resolution proof of a trivial Resolution lower bound by polynomial-size \(\mathbf{TC}^0\) or quasipolynomial-size \(\mathbf{AC}^0\) circuits. They prove the Atserias–Müller lower bound in quasipolynomial-size constant-depth Frege for every size parameter, and show that the Proof Analysis Problem is NP-hard under quasipolynomial-time reductions for any system that p-simulates depth-2 Frege. Separately, Riis proves linear degree lower bounds for Nullstellensatz and Polynomial Calculus, which give a four-way classification of uniform polynomial consequences over fields of positive characteristic.

Two papers this week ask how much a proof system can prove about proofs, and how hard proofs in a weak system can be forced to be. One moves a central Resolution lower bound down into constant-depth Frege. The other replaces logarithmic degree bounds with linear ones across a whole classification.

What was known

In 2019 Atserias and Müller showed that Resolution cannot be automated unless \(\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}\). Their reduction maps a CNF \(\varphi\) to a formula \(\mathrm{Ref}_s(\varphi)\), which says that \(\varphi\) has a Resolution refutation of length \(s\). If \(\varphi\) is satisfiable, \(\mathrm{Ref}_s(\varphi)\) has short refutations. If \(\varphi\) is unsatisfiable, every refutation is exponentially long. Pudlák had asked when proof systems can prove such lower bounds about themselves, and called that question more fundamental than automatability.

Last year Arteche, Atserias, de Rezende and Khaniki made the question computational. They defined the Proof Analysis Problem. You are given \(\varphi\) and a proof of \(\neg\mathrm{Ref}_s(\varphi)\), and you must decide whether \(\varphi\) is satisfiable, or find an assignment if it is. They gave a polynomial-time extraction algorithm for Resolution proofs and formalised it in \(\mathsf{PV}_1\), so Extended Frege proves the Atserias–Müller bound. They also showed the problem is NP-hard for Extended Frege. Their algorithm looked inherently sequential. Li, Li and Ren (STOC 2026) obtained quasipolynomial constant-depth Frege formalisations of some Resolution lower bounds, but only for \(s=2^{\Omega(n)}\). In that regime the formula is padded to exponential size, and brute force becomes cheap.

The classification exists, and no algorithm can say which case you are in.

What is new

Arteche, de Rezende, Khaniki and Risse [arXiv:2611.08305] give an extraction algorithm in polynomial-size \(\mathbf{TC}^0\) and in quasipolynomial-size \(\mathbf{AC}^0\). They prove the correctness of the \(\mathbf{AC}^0\) version in the bounded-arithmetic theory \(\mathsf{T}^4_2(\alpha)\), which corresponds to depth-4 Frege. Translated into propositional form, this gives quasipolynomial-size constant-depth Frege proofs of the Atserias–Müller lower bound for every \(s\), including \(s=n^{O(1)}\). That is progress on Pudlák's question, though it does not settle it, since the proofs are quasipolynomial rather than polynomial.

Three consequences follow. The Proof Analysis Problem is NP-hard, under quasipolynomial-time reductions, for every system that p-simulates depth-2 Frege. So the basic Atserias–Müller route, using Ref formulas for Resolution, cannot show that automating constant-depth Frege is hard. Next, for each \(d\ge3\), the system \(\mathsf{PK}_{d+1/2}\) is automatable in quasipolynomial time on Ref formulas if and only if it is automatable on all CNFs. Finally, Haken's pigeonhole lower bound gets quasipolynomial depth-2 proofs in the polynomial-size regime.

The technique replaces the earlier greedy restriction with the original random restriction of Atserias and Müller. That restriction kills every clause of block-width above \(c\log|\pi|\), so the algorithm never has to build it. It simply ignores wide clauses. For each narrow clause, in parallel, it tries every possible pointer structure for the Delayer's bookkeeping, at cost \(s^{O(\log|\pi|)}\), and checks consistency with constant-depth circuits. Correctness of the old sequential algorithm guarantees that some clause must encode a satisfying assignment. To remove the quasipolynomial guessing, the authors add dummy layers to the Delayer's grid, which gives the \(\mathbf{TC}^0\) version. To formalise the argument, they derandomise the restriction with \(k\)-wise independence and approximate counting carried out inside the theory.

Linear degree, four types

Riis [arXiv:2611.07280] works with uniform families: systems of polynomial equations generated by fixed bounded-degree patterns as the universe size \(n\) grows, for example the algebraic translations of first-order sentences. His 2008–2009 classification sorted refutations of such families into four types. The degree lower bounds behind it were only \(\Omega(\log n)\) for Nullstellensatz and \(\Omega(\log\log n)\) for Polynomial Calculus. He now proves linear lower bounds for both systems, and extends the result from refutations to the derivation of arbitrary uniform targets.

Beyond a finite initial range, exactly one of four things happens:

  • T1: degree stays bounded.
  • T2: degree is at least linear in \(n\).
  • T3: a periodic mixture, with low degree on some residue classes and linear degree on the rest.
  • T4: infinitely many recurring finite degrees, with finite \(\Omega(n)\) peaks at arbitrarily large sizes.

Both systems assign the same type. Telling any two types apart is undecidable, even for refutations. The bounds, the classification and the undecidability results are formalised in Lean.

The engine is periodicity. For a fixed degree budget \(d\), whether a target can be derived within that budget becomes periodic in \(n\) after an onset linear in \(d\), with period the least power of the characteristic exceeding a label budget linear in \(d\). A degree that does not recur can only occur before its onset; turning that statement around gives the linear bound. An example with \(\Theta(n)\) degree shows that linear is optimal in general. The exponential size bounds count every coefficient, zeros included. Sparse representations are a different measure, and the paper says so.

Why it matters, and what is open

For a reader who works on CSP, Riis's result has a familiar shape: a classification of uniform families by structure. The difference is instructive. The classification exists, and no algorithm can say which case you are in.

The Frege paper redraws the map of automatability. Hardness for constant-depth Frege now needs new ideas, or Ref formulas built for stronger systems than Resolution. A polynomial-time version of the NP-hardness reduction would follow from Pudlák's open problem: polynomial-size \(\mathbf{AC}^0\)-Frege proofs of Resolution lower bounds. Whether the \(\mathbf{TC}^0\) algorithm can be proved correct in the matching theory \(\mathsf{VTC}^0\) is also unresolved. The current argument reasons about objects of quasipolynomial size, which that theory cannot handle.

THE LONG VIEW

Splitting the variables: how the hyperclique wall was built

Alman, Li, Pant, Pratt and Williams count \(k\)-hypercliques in \((k-1)\)-uniform hypergraphs in \(O(n^{k-\varepsilon_k})\) time, giving Max-\(k\)SAT in \(2^{(1-\delta)n}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(m,n)\) for every \(k\); this caps two decades of reductions that began with Williams's three-block Max-2SAT algorithm, and the result is a preprint, not yet refereed.

The template

Every algorithm in this story repeats one move. In 1969 Strassen found a 7-term decomposition of \(2\times 2\) matrix multiplication, and recursion turned it into circuits of size \(O(n^{\log_2 7})\) [S1]. In 1978 Itai and Rodeh applied fast matrix multiplication to triangles: if \(A\) is the adjacency matrix of a graph, \(\mathrm{tr}(A^3)\) is six times the number of triangles [S1]. In 1985 Nešetřil and Poljak reduced \(k\)-Clique to triangle detection by grouping vertices into tuples. They detected \(k\)-cliques in \(O(n^{\omega k/3})\) time for \(k\) divisible by 3 [S2, S3, S4].

The recipe is short. Find a structure whose count is a trilinear form, then evaluate it with matrix multiplication.

Splitting the variables

In 2004 Ryan Williams applied the recipe to exponential-time problems (ICALP 2004; Theoret. Comput. Sci. 2005) [S5, S6]. Split the \(n\) variables into three blocks. List the \(2^{n/3}\) partial assignments of each block as vertices. Weight the edges so that a triangle's weight equals the number of clauses its full assignment satisfies. Then guess the weight on each of the three edge classes, which gives \(O(m^3)\) choices, and count ordinary triangles.

This counts the optimal solutions of Max-2SAT and Max-Cut in \(O(m^3\cdot 2^{\omega n/3})\) time. It was the first exponential improvement over trivial search [S5]. With \(\omega<2.376\) the base is about \(1.732\) [S7]. Koivisto cut the polynomial overhead to roughly \(nm\) in 2005 [S7].

Three blocks are not enough

A 3-clause can touch three blocks, so its contribution no longer fits on an edge. The natural repair uses \(\ell\ge k+1\) blocks and \(k\)-uniform hyperedges. With only \(k\) blocks, one hyperedge covers a whole assignment and nothing is saved. According to Lincoln, Vassilevska Williams and Williams, Williams's 2007 thesis reduced Max-3SAT to finding 4-hypercliques in 3-uniform hypergraphs. An \(O(n^{4-\varepsilon})\) algorithm there would give Max-3SAT in \(O(2^{(1-\delta)n})\) [S2]. Alman et al. instead date the question to the 2005 paper [S1]; the sources disagree.

In a 2010 note Williams asked directly whether the order-3 product could be computed in \(O(n^{4-\varepsilon})\) time. LVW18 record unsuccessful attempts by several researchers [S2]. The product itself was older: Mesner and Bhattacharya had defined a ternary product of 3-tensors in 1990 [S2].

Meanwhile Max-3SAT moved only slightly. Alman, Chan and Williams (FOCS 2016) reached \(2^{n-n/O(\log^2 n \log^2\log n)}\) [S3], which LVW18 described as the fastest Max-\(k\)SAT algorithm [S2]. The saving was \(2^{o(n)}\).

The conjectures harden

In FOCS 2015 Abboud, Backurs and Vassilevska Williams showed that faster context-free parsing would imply a breakthrough \(k\)-Clique algorithm. They treated the optimality of the matrix-multiplication approach as a conjecture of graph algorithms and parameterized complexity [S8]. LVW18 then wrote it down as the \(k\)-Clique Hypothesis, a lower bound of \(n^{\omega k/3-o(1)}\), citing that paper [S2].

The SODA 2018 paper of Lincoln, Vassilevska Williams and Williams stated two more hypotheses [S2]:

  • Hyperclique. For \(\ell>k>2\), finding an \(\ell\)-hyperclique in a \(k\)-uniform hypergraph needs \(n^{\ell-o(1)}\) time.
  • Max-\(k\)SAT. Max-\(k\)SAT needs \(2^{n-o(n)}\) time.

It also gave a reason to believe them. The \(k\)-wise product has border rank exactly \(n^{k+1}\), which the authors said “completely rules out the arithmetic circuit approach for \(k>2\)”. A faster algorithm, they concluded, “must use radically different techniques” [S2]. From the hypotheses they derived, among other bounds, \(m^{k/(k-1)-o(1)}\) lower bounds for Shortest Cycle in sparse directed graphs [S2].

Later work leaned on the same assumption. It was used for induced 4-cycles, hypercycles, lowest common ancestors in DAGs, database joins, CSPs and Klee's measure problem [S3]. At STOC 2026 Dudek, Fischer, Gokaj, Jin, Künnemann, Mao and Redžić added the \(k\)-AP Hypothesis. It says that for \(k\ge 4\), no \(O(n^{2-\varepsilon})\) algorithm detects a nontrivial length-\(k\) arithmetic progression in a subset of \(\{0,\ldots,n\}\). They linked it to hyperclique detection [S9, S1]. The case \(k=3\) is excluded because FFT handles it in near-linear time [S1].

Where the barrier leaked

The rank argument rests on a 1973 theorem of Strassen: for three-mode tensors, rank characterises multiplicative complexity [S1]. With four or more modes, circuits can have several layers of products, and rank stops bounding size. The standard example is \((\sum_i x_i z_i)(\sum_j y_j w_j)\). It has a linear-size circuit, yet its flattening is an identity matrix, so its rank is \(n^2\) [S1].

Naslund's partition rank (2020) gave a finer measure [S1]. In 2026 Brand, Kaski and Wang, and a larger group including Curticapean, proposed partition-rank notions meant to extend Strassen's bound to more modes [S1]. Chan et al. note that this work identified the weakness of the rank argument but produced no new hyperclique lower bounds [S3]. The LVW18 theorem remains true. What failed is the conclusion drawn from it.

Alman, Li, Pant, Pratt and Williams [arXiv:2611.05082] give uniform arithmetic circuits of size \(O(n^{k-\varepsilon_k})\) for the \(k\)-hyperclique tensor in \((k-1)\)-uniform hypergraphs, with \(\varepsilon_k=2^{-\Theta(2^k)}\) [S1]. They feed this into the split-and-list reduction with \(k+1\) blocks. The result is Max-\(k\)SAT, #Max-\(k\)SAT and Max-Degree-\(k\)CSP in \(2^{(1-\delta_k)n}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n,m)\), with \(\delta_k=\varepsilon_{k+1}/(k+1)\), and \(k\)-AP detection in \(O(n^{2-2\varepsilon_k/k})\) [S1]. The paper states that its main result was obtained by ChatGPT 6 Pro, after the authors suggested the algebraic approach [S1].

One day later, Chan, Itakura, Ma and Yu independently gave an \(O(n^{4-\varepsilon})\) algorithm for 4-hypercliques in 3-uniform hypergraphs. They use only rectangular matrix multiplication and obtain Max-3SAT in \(2^{(1-\varepsilon/4+o(1))n}\) [S3].

The claimed October refutations of ETH did not already give these bounds, because no sparsification lemma is known for Max-\(k\)SAT [S1]. In the same week, Vassilevska Williams and Zamir [arXiv:2611.09764] claim an \(n^{(\omega/3-c)k}\) algorithm for \(k\)-Clique for large \(k\). That would also overturn the Nešetřil–Poljak bound.

None of this has been refereed. A saving of \(2^{-\Theta(2^k)}\) in the exponent refutes a conjecture; it does not yet speed up a solver. How large the savings can be for each \(k\) is open.

The technique: Split-and-list reduction to (hyper)clique counting

The reduction turns an exponential search over assignments into a polynomial problem on a graph with \(N=2^{n/3}\) vertices per part. Fast matrix multiplication then beats \(2^n\).

For Max-2SAT, split the variables into blocks \(A,B,C\). Each partial assignment of a block becomes a vertex. Charge every clause to one edge class covering the blocks it touches; a 2-clause touches at most two blocks. Then the weight of triangle \((a,b,c)\) is the number of clauses satisfied by the full assignment. Guess a weight \(g_e\) for each edge class, and let \(M_e\) be the 0/1 matrix of edges with that weight. Then \(\mathrm{tr}(M_{AB}M_{BC}M_{CA})\) counts the assignments with that weight profile.

Toy instance. Take one variable per block: \(x_1\in A\), \(x_2\in B\), \(x_3\in C\). The clauses are \(C_1=(x_1\vee x_2)\), \(C_2=(\neg x_2\vee x_3)\), \(C_3=(\neg x_1\vee\neg x_3)\) and \(C_4=(x_2)\). Charge \(C_1,C_4\) to \(AB\), \(C_2\) to \(BC\) and \(C_3\) to \(CA\). The edge weights are:

  • \(w_{AB}(x_1,x_2)\): \((0,0)\mapsto 0\), \((0,1)\mapsto 2\), \((1,0)\mapsto 1\), \((1,1)\mapsto 2\).
  • \(w_{BC}(x_2,x_3)\): \(1,1,0,1\) in the same order.
  • \(w_{CA}(x_3,x_1)\): \(1,1,1,0\) in the same order.

The assignment \((0,1,1)\) gives \(2+1+1=4\) and satisfies every clause. Summing the traces over all triples with \(g_1+g_2+g_3=t\) gives 3 assignments at \(t=2\), 4 at \(t=3\) and 1 at \(t=4\). This agrees with brute force, so the method counts optima, not merely detects them.

There are \(O(m^3)\) guesses, each costing \(O(N^{\omega})\), for \(O(m^3\cdot 2^{\omega n/3})\) in total. For \(k\)-clauses the same construction needs \(k+1\) blocks and \(k\)-uniform hyperedges. A fast \((k+1)\)-hyperclique counter then plays the part of matrix multiplication, and that counter is what this week's paper supplies.

Inventory After the Refutations

The late column. The week's papers set against each other, looking for the pattern nobody wrote down. Plain words. No favourites.

The week had three refutations and one replacement. The replacement is weaker than what it replaces.

Alman, Li, Pant, Pratt and Williams [arXiv:2611.05082] count \(k\)-hypercliques in \((k-1)\)-uniform hypergraphs in \(O(n^{k-\varepsilon_k})\) time for every fixed \(k\). The route is a uniform family of arithmetic circuits for the hyperclique tensor, the higher-order relative of the matrix multiplication tensor. The hyperclique conjecture goes. So does exhaustive search for Max-\(k\)SAT, which drops to \(2^{(1-\varepsilon)n}\). So does the \(n^2\) barrier for detecting \(k\)-term arithmetic progressions. The abstract does not state \(\varepsilon_k\). It says only that it is positive.

Vassilevska Williams and Zamir [arXiv:2611.09764] do the same to \(k\)-Clique: \(n^{(\omega/3-c)k}\) for large \(k\), with \(c\) independent of both \(\omega\) and \(k\). The conjecture fails even if \(\omega=2\). The algorithm also counts weighted cliques.

Those two are algorithms. The third refutation is a premise. Brunner, Demaine and the MIT Hardness Group [arXiv:2611.02650] open by stating that ETH is false and proceed from there. The refutation itself is not on this week's list. What they offer in its place is Stearns and Hunt's SH, proved equivalent to Weak ETH, which rules out \(2^{n^{1-\varepsilon}}\) algorithms for 3SAT. Reductions still carry hardness. The cost shows in the exponent. A blowup \(N=s^b\) used to rule out \(2^{o(N^{1/b})}\). It now rules out \(2^{N^{1/b-\varepsilon}}\). The framework survives. The sharpest bounds do not.

Now the other direction. Chukhin [arXiv:2611.06231] shows that exhaustive search for hyperclique is hard to beat in constant depth. Depth-\(h\) circuits need \(m^{\Omega(\ell)}\) gates for \(\ell\le m^{1/h-\varepsilon}\). Rossman's clique bound extends from constant-size to polynomial-size cliques. Through a reduction of Choudhury, Limaye, Sreenivasaiah and Srinivasan, the \(k\)-OV conjecture becomes a theorem for constant-depth circuits in polylogarithmic dimension.

Same week, same problem, opposite conclusions, no contradiction. The fast algorithms are built the way matrix multiplication is built. Nothing in their abstracts claims constant depth, and Chukhin's bounds say they cannot have it. The conjecture did not die everywhere. It moved into a model where it can be proved.

That is the drift. Hypotheses once stated for all algorithms are coming back in two forms. One is a theorem about a restricted model. The other is a weaker hypothesis with a softer exponent. Li's attention note [hexagon:2611.00132v1] records where one lower bound now stands. The quadratic bound for attention rests on low-dimensional Orthogonal Vectors, which no open satisfiability hypothesis is known to imply. This week's only unconditional support for that problem is Chukhin's \(\Omega(n^2)\) wires for depth-three 2-OV in logarithmic dimension. It covers depth three.

The Hexagon queue shows the opposite habit. AI-assisted manuscripts derive corollaries from the claimed subexponential #SAT engines: Eulerian circuit counting [hexagon:2611.00088v1], exact Clifford+T amplitudes [hexagon:2611.00090v1], the parity of \(\pi(x)\) [hexagon:2611.00116v1]. They are stated as conditional, which is correct. The Eulerian result rests on a Hamiltonian-cycle result that rests on the claim. A conditional result is worth exactly its condition, and stacking does not raise the value. The more useful entry from the same source is the barrier paper [hexagon:2611.00149v1]. It asks what those engines cannot do.

The week also showed what breaking a barrier can cost. Curticapean [arXiv:2611.03636] computes \(n\times n\) permanents in \(O^*((2-\varepsilon)^n)\) operations over every commutative ring, with \(\varepsilon=10^{-40}\). Ryser's \(2^n\) falls and a problem from Knuth is closed. Read it as a discount coupon. Against \(2^n\) the saving is a factor of about \(e^{-n\varepsilon/2}\). It halves the bill once the matrix has roughly \(1.4\times10^{40}\) rows. Nobody will redeem it. It still settles the question: \(2^n\) was a habit, not a law. The hyperclique and clique papers have the same shape, with exponents the abstracts leave unstated.

So the inventory is short. Three conjectures fell this week. One returned as a theorem, at constant depth.
