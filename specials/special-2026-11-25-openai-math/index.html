One drop claims VP≠VNP and Smale's ninth

Ten AI-written, unrefereed manuscripts claim that VP and VNP differ over every field, give superpolynomial circuit lower bounds for the permanent, settle the Erdős–Rado sunflower conjecture, and give a strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programming, each with a Lean formalisation of narrower scope.

The drop

OpenAI math has released ten manuscripts dated 23 and 24 November 2026. An AI system wrote all of them, none is refereed, and all ten carry a Lean formalisation. They fall into three families.

Euclidean tensor geometry

The largest family is built on [oai:euclidean-geometry-in-the-asymptotic-20261124]. It assigns costs to tensors whose indices sit in a real Hilbert space, so that a bit shared among sites costs the mean width of their convex hull. Its abstract says the companion papers use these costs. [oai:paths-trees-and-grids-separate-valiants-20261124] claims \(\mathsf{VP}\ne\mathsf{VNP}\) over every field, witnessed by a grid state sum that needs circuits of size \(n^{\Omega((\log n)^{1/12})}\). On trees and paths the same construction is said to separate circuits from algebraic branching programs, and branching programs from formulas. [oai:circuit-lower-bounds-for-hamiltonian-20261124] claims \(\exp(c(\log N)^2/(\log\log N)^2)\) gate lower bounds for the directed Hamiltonian cycle polynomial, and for the permanent outside characteristic two. [oai:an-exponential-bound-for-sunflowers-20261124] claims the fixed-petal sunflower conjecture. Three further papers claim exact logarithmic exponents for Fourier transforms, for integer multiplication on tapes and for matrix multiplication. The integer paper ties its lower bound to a Weak ETH for 3-SAT. The matrix paper's abstract calls the \(9/4\) upper bound known, which the desk's scoring treats as a reason for caution.

Linear programming

[oai:strongly-polynomial-linear-programming-20261124] claims a deterministic strongly polynomial LP algorithm, which would answer Smale's ninth problem, and extends it to convex quadratic programming and mean-payoff games. [oai:superpolynomial-graph-diameter-of-20261124] claims to disprove the polynomial Hirsch conjecture.

The 23 November preprint

[oai:vp-and-vnp-are-different-20261123] states that the 24 November papers subsume it.

What is checked

The Lean scope notes are narrower than the manuscripts. For the permanent paper, Lean proves eventual superpolynomial exact lower bounds, as formalised. The quantitative and approximate forms are not covered. For the grid paper, it proves the three strict inclusions for the exact classes, not the border statements. For sunflowers, it proves that some base \(C_r\) exists; the dependence on \(r\) is in the manuscript only. For matrix multiplication, it covers division-free circuits for formal identities. Each formalisation checks the statement as written in Lean. Whether those definitions match the usual ones is a question for readers.

VALIANT'S HIERARCHY

One state sum on three graphs is claimed to split VF, VBP, VP and VNP

An AI-written preprint claims that VP differs from VNP over every field, and that the state sum on grids, binary trees and paths also separates circuits from branching programs and branching programs from formulas.

What is claimed

[oai:paths-trees-and-grids-separate-valiants-20261124] states that \(\mathsf{VF} \subsetneq \mathsf{VBP} \subsetneq \mathsf{VP} \subsetneq \mathsf{VNP}\) over every field. All three witnesses are one polynomial, the state sum. Each edge of a graph takes one of \(q\) labels. Each vertex holds a table of independent variables. A labelling picks one table entry per vertex, and the state sum adds the products over all labellings.

On the \(n\times n\) grid with \(n\) states per edge the state sum is in VNP and, the paper states, needs circuits of size \(n^{\Omega((\log n)^{1/12})}\). On a complete binary tree it is in VP and needs algebraic branching programs of size \(n^{\Omega(\sqrt{\log\log n})}\). On a path it is iterated matrix multiplication, in VBP, and needs formulas of size \(n^{\Omega(\log\log n)}\). The bounds are stated in degree-component border complexity, with arbitrary constants and free affine inputs. The determinant is claimed to get the same formula bound. The introduction also announces a classification: formulas, ABPs and circuits give polynomial size exactly for bounded degree and component size, bounded cutwidth, and bounded treewidth with bounded degree. This preprint subsumes the earlier [oai:vp-and-vnp-are-different-20261123].

Every one of the three lower bounds runs through one inequality: summing a few cheap tensors cannot cost much more than the dearest of them.

What it would mean

If it holds, Valiant's algebraic analogue of P versus NP is settled in every characteristic, and the formula versus branching-program question goes with it. CSP readers will recognise the witness as a partition function of a constraint network with an equality constraint on every edge, and the classification is phrased in treewidth and cutwidth.

What the paper leans on

The engine is the Euclidean pricing theorem of the companion [oai:euclidean-geometry-in-the-asymptotic-20261124], built on Strassen's asymptotic spectrum. Tensor indices are placed at points in space; sharing a \(q\)-valued label between two points costs their distance times \(\log q\). The two ends of each edge go to opposite unit vectors on an axis of their own, so the state sum's coefficient tensor costs exactly \(2ew\), with \(w=\log q\).

A small computation, the paper argues, writes that tensor as a sum of few terms, each of low rank across some cut. If many edges cross the cut, moving one supply point towards them saves wire length faster than the low-rank link costs, so each term is cheaper than the target. Every one of the three lower bounds runs through one inequality: summing a few cheap tensors cannot cost much more than the dearest of them. Grids supply balanced cuts with many crossing edges via a Valiant–Skyum–Berkowitz–Rackoff style decomposition. For trees, an ABP lets one fix the cut's size, chosen through an edge-isoperimetric count after Bharadwaj and Chandran. For paths, formula splits are charged at several scales at once using Gaussian mean width. The bounds are first shown for tiny graphs with many states, \(2\le d\le (\log q)^{1/3}\), then moved to the large witnesses by projection.

What has been checked

The drop lists a Lean development that, in its own words, “proves all three strict inclusions for the exact arithmetic classes over every field”. So the separations of the exact classes are proved in that formalised sense. The listing does not claim the border versions, the stated exponents, the determinant bound or the classification. It does not say whether the companion pricing theorem is formalised or assumed, and the scope document should settle that. Nothing here is refereed. The text supplied to this paper was cut off early in Section 6, so the path argument and Section 7 were not read in full.

What to look at first

Lemma 3.2, where one Euclidean price controls summands whose cuts differ, and the sum bound (3.1) with the Zariski-closed sublevels it imports; these carry both the circuit bounds and the border versions. Then Lemma 4.2's decomposition into balanced cuts, and the placement of leaves in Lemma 6.3. A result of this size resting on a single new invariant from an unrefereed companion is where checking should start.

ARITHMETIC CIRCUIT LOWER BOUNDS

A claimed superpolynomial circuit lower bound for Hamiltonian cycle and the permanent, over every field

An AI-written, unrefereed paper claims that arithmetic circuits for the directed Hamiltonian cycle polynomial need \(\exp(c(\log N)^2/(\log\log N)^2)\) gates over every field, and for the permanent outside characteristic two, even in a border model.

What is claimed

[oai:circuit-lower-bounds-for-hamiltonian-20261124] states its main result as Theorem 1.1. There is an absolute constant \(c>0\) with the following property. For each characteristic \(\chi\), including zero, and every order \(N \ge N_0(\chi)\), any arithmetic circuit for the directed Hamiltonian cycle polynomial \(HC_N\) needs at least \(\exp(c(\log N)^2/(\log\log N)^2)\) gates over every field of that characteristic.

The same bound is claimed for the permanent when \(\chi \ne 2\). The paper notes that this exception is forced, since in characteristic two the permanent equals the determinant.

The paper says either outcome yields a lower bound, and that no classification of characteristics is needed.

The measure is degree-component border size. Each gate is a binary addition, subtraction or multiplication, and intermediate degrees are unrestricted. The target need only lie in the Zariski closure of the degree-\(N\) components of circuit outputs, so exact circuits and ordinary border circuits are special cases. The paper adds that the bound already holds on sparse instances. For Hamiltonian cycle it suffices to keep at most three incoming and three outgoing edges per vertex (Corollary 3.5).

What it would mean

Both polynomials are VNP-complete in the stated characteristics. The paper therefore concludes that VNP is not contained in border VP over any field.

It also uses the polynomial-size circuit for the determinant to claim a superpolynomial lower bound on the matrix order of determinantal representations. It contrasts this with the quadratic and cubic bounds it cites from earlier work. The earlier bound it cites for unrestricted exact computation of the permanent over \(\mathbb{C}\) is \(\Omega(N^2\log\log N)\).

A companion paper, [oai:paths-trees-and-grids-separate-valiants-20261124], separates VP from VNP with a grid polynomial. This paper claims a larger exponent for the two classical complete families.

What the paper leans on

The argument splits on a geometric property of the characteristic. Tensor “prices” drawn from Strassen's asymptotic spectrum turn wires into distances. A characteristic “admits ports” if bounded-degree regular graphs satisfy a uniform nonlinear Poincaré inequality in every resulting space. The paper says either outcome yields a lower bound, and that no classification of characteristics is needed.

Without ports (Part I). A small circuit is broken into at most \(2^d\) terms, each of low rank across its own balanced cut, using the balanced-gate idea from depth reduction. One placement of the rows makes every such term cheap. An explicit \(\{0,1\}\) tensor, built from exterior products of minors, costs nearly the maximum at that placement. This gives size \(n^{\Omega(d/\sqrt{\log d})}\) at fixed degree \(d\).

With ports (Part II). Compression identities would let a program simulate its own count of computation paths, which yields a contradiction. Border circuits are handled with residues and the Cartier operator, and characteristic zero by specialisation to a prime. In this case the paper claims the stronger bound \(2^{N^{\varepsilon_\chi}}\).

Several key facts are imported from the companion paper [oai:euclidean-geometry-in-the-asymptotic-20261124]. These include the flat law for mean width, the convexity of prices and the definition of ports. The result therefore depends on that paper as well.

What has been checked

Nothing in the drop is refereed. According to the drop, “The Lean development proves eventual superpolynomial exact circuit lower bounds for the directed Hamiltonian cycle polynomial over every field and the permanent outside characteristic two.”

That statement concerns exact circuits and superpolynomial growth. The catalogue entry does not say that any of the following are formalised:

  • the quantitative exponent;
  • the border and degree-component model;
  • the sparse-graph corollary;
  • the \(2^{N^{\varepsilon_\chi}}\) bound.

The scope document would settle whether the Lean development includes the geometry imported from the companion paper or takes parts of it as hypotheses. This summary rests on the first three sections of the text; the rest was not read.

What to look at first

Lemma 3.3, the balanced-cut decomposition, is short and can be checked by hand. Next come Lemma 2.2, the bound on the price of a shared label, and Theorems 6.1, 7.1 and 11.2, which supply the placement and the witness tensor. For Part II, Theorems 12.1 and 12.2 and the residue argument of Sections 16–19 carry the border case.

Readers who want to know what has been checked should open the formalisation scope first. A claim this large, from an unrefereed source, is best read against what the Lean files actually state.

LINEAR PROGRAMMING

A claimed strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programming, with games as a by-product

An AI-written manuscript claims a deterministic algorithm that solves linear programs with real data in a number of steps polynomial in the numbers of variables and constraints alone, answering Smale's ninth problem.

What is claimed

The manuscript [oai:strongly-polynomial-linear-programming-20261124] states, as Theorem 1, that for arbitrary real \(A\), \(b\), \(c\) a deterministic algorithm decides \(\max\{c^\top x : Ax \le b\}\) in a number of steps polynomial in \(m\) and \(n\). It returns one of three certificates: a strictly complementary primal–dual pair, a feasible point with an improving ray, or a Farkas vector \(y\) with support at most \(1 + \operatorname{rank} A\). The model is a uniform real machine with exact addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, comparison and copying; control work on indices is charged at bit cost. On rational input, every intermediate number is claimed to have bit length polynomial in the binary input length. The text says the arithmetic result extends to every ordered field.

The paper also claims strongly polynomial algorithms for monotone linear complementarity and convex quadratic programming, a fixed-point algorithm for max-norm contractions, and exact values and optimal strategies for turn-based mean-payoff games: strongly polynomial for deterministic games, polynomial bit time for stochastic games with rational data, including simple stochastic reachability games.

The description does not mention arbitrary real data, strict complementarity, quadratic programming, contractions or games, so those claims rest on the manuscript alone.

What it would mean

If correct, this settles the question raised by Megiddo and Tarjan and posed by Smale. The game corollaries would place several long-standing open problems, such as polynomial-time solvability of simple stochastic games, in polynomial time. A result of this reach from a single unrefereed source warrants close checking before anyone builds on it.

What the paper leans on

The idea is to decide, for each coordinate of the homogenised slack space, whether a nonnegative witness lives in the subspace \(C\) or in its orthogonal complement. A witness entry may be tiny, so the algorithm rescales coordinates by reciprocal factors, which it calls prices, to bring entries into view. At each price vector an ordinary bit-polynomial LP returns labels that are reliable only at the largest scaled entries. A search procedure (Theorem 2, “extremal prices”) combines these partial labels, through a weighted-experts argument with recursive child calls, into prices whose signs reveal every side. Coordinates are then zeroed one at a time until the witness lies on a line and is recovered exactly.

Keeping each query affordable is the job of a calibration procedure (Theorem 3). It replaces logarithms of subdeterminant magnitudes with short substitutes that preserve the relevant comparisons. The paper extends Frank–Tardos rounding to one minor at a time, uses lattice reduction, and bounds the number of restarts by a counting argument on integer polynomials in the matrix entries. The text credits the recursive search to work of Soleymani and Farina and earlier game algorithms, and relates the price choice to balanced points of Chen, Li and Yannakakis. It cites the companion paper [oai:superpolynomial-graph-diameter-of-20261124] for context on edge walks, not as a dependency.

What has been checked

The drop lists a Lean formalisation. In its own words: for rational data with at least one constraint and one variable, Lean proves strongly polynomial feasibility and optimisation on fixed rational RAM programs, with instruction count polynomial in the dimensions and stored rationals of bit length polynomial in the input length. In the finite-optimum case the formalised certificate gives primal and dual solutions of equal value. The description does not mention arbitrary real data, strict complementarity, quadratic programming, contractions or games, so those claims rest on the manuscript alone. Nothing here is refereed.

What to look at first

The calibration theorem and the compression of minor magnitudes (Sections 4 and 5) carry the strongly polynomial claim, including the case where a comparison involves an exceptionally large logarithm. The text handles that by comparing products of powers of minors, and justifies the bit bound only on rational data, so the real-data case is worth tracing. Next, the wealth accounting in the recursive search of Section 6, which must rule out failure at the root. Finally, compare the Lean RAM statement with Theorem 1 to see exactly which model and data the formal proof covers.

SUNFLOWER CONJECTURE

An AI-written manuscript claims the Erdős–Rado fixed-petal sunflower conjecture, priced by tensor geometry

The paper claims that for every \(r \ge 3\), any family of more than \(B_r^k\) distinct sets of size at most \(k\) contains an \(r\)-sunflower, with \(\log B_r\) polylogarithmic in \(r\) and no dependence on \(k\) in the base.

What is claimed

[oai:an-exponential-bound-for-sunflowers-20261124] states, as Theorem 1.1, that for each integer \(r \ge 3\) there is a constant \(B_r\) with the following property. Every family of distinct sets of size at most \(k\) that contains no \(r\)-sunflower has at most \(B_r^k\) members, for every \(k \ge 1\). An \(r\)-sunflower is \(r\) sets whose pairwise intersections all equal one core. The paper gives \(B_r = 2^{CH^4L^{5/2}}\), with \(H=\log_2(2r)\), \(L=\log_2(2H)\) and \(C\) absolute. That is, \(\log B_r = O((\log r)^4(\log\log 2r)^{5/2})\).

This is the fixed-petal conjecture, in which the base may depend arbitrarily on \(r\). The paper notes that the base must be at least \(r-1\). It also says the stronger hope of a base \(O(r)\) remains open.

In a sunflower-free family only repeated words get through, so independent labels have become one shared label.

What it would mean

The strongest earlier bounds the paper cites have the form \((Cr\log(k+1))^k\), due to Bell, Chueluecha and Warnke. They followed work by Alweiss, Lovett, Wu and Zhang and by Rao. A base free of \(k\) would settle a question Erdős stated explicitly. The paper points to two links of interest to complexity theorists. Sunflower lemmas enter Razborov's lower bounds for monotone circuits. Alon, Shpilka and Umans showed that the fixed-petal conjecture rules out a group construction for matrix multiplication proposed by Coppersmith and Winograd.

What the paper leans on

The first step is a random colouring. It reduces \(k\)-uniform families to families of words, losing at most a factor \(e^k\). This is the transversal reduction of Alon, Shpilka and Umans.

Then come tensors. Each of \(r\) groups of sites picks a word independently. In every column, local operations kill any partial repetition, a symbol that appears twice but not in every row. Constant columns survive. In a sunflower-free family only repeated words get through, so independent labels have become one shared label. A spectral point on tensors puts a price on this, and the price only falls under local maps. Sharing then costs more than the column tests can pay, unless the family is small.

The prices come from a Euclidean placement of the sites, in which a shared bit costs a normalised mean width. The hard part is to keep each test's cost bounded however large the alphabet. The paper's equality test pairs labels as \(\{x, x+p\}\) and subtracts the single false match this produces. Part I uses a planar layout and already gives \(\log B_r = O(r^5)\). Part II tests a whole column at once to reach the polylogarithmic dependence. It relies on positive characteristic for a degree constraint.

The main outside dependency is Theorem 2.2, on Euclidean prices, which the paper quotes from its reference [19] on the Euclidean geometry of the asymptotic spectrum. The drop's companion paper [oai:euclidean-geometry-in-the-asymptotic-20261124] names an exponential sunflower bound among its applications. Neither manuscript is refereed.

What has been checked

The catalogue says the Lean development proves that for each \(r \ge 3\) there exists a constant \(C_r\) giving the bound \(C_r^k\) for finite families of \(k\)-element sets. In its own words, “the manuscript gives the quantitative dependence of the base on \(r\)”. So the polylogarithmic estimate for \(\log B_r\) is not in the formalisation.

The formal statement concerns uniform families. The paper passes to sets of size at most \(k\) by padding each set with private elements. The material available here does not say whether the formal development contains the Euclidean spectral point or takes it as given. The trimmed text read for this piece stops early in Section 6, so Part II's argument was not read in full.

What to look at first

Start with Proposition 2.1. It is short, and it holds the whole comparison between independent and shared labels. Next, read Theorem 3.1 and the planar layout of Section 3, which on their own claim the qualitative statement. Then go to Theorem 2.2 in the companion paper, on which every price in the argument depends.

The formalisation scope document deserves a careful read. A qualitative Lean theorem is only as informative as the definitions and imported results it rests on. One sunflower bound that skips past several decades of incremental progress, arriving in the same drop as other long-standing claims, is reason to read slowly.
